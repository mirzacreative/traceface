C2PA Content Credentials: Verify an Image's Origin

November 19, 2026 · Muhammad Atif Iqbal

You have an image and a question: where did it come from? Was it taken by a camera, made by an AI generator, or edited somewhere along the way? Until recently, there was no reliable way to answer that from the file itself — you had to guess from the pixels, or trust whoever posted it. Content Credentials change that, at least for the images that carry them. They are a tamper-evident record, attached to the file, that says what the image is and what has happened to it. This guide explains what C2PA Content Credentials are, which cameras and apps attach them, how to check an image's credentials step by step, what a credential can and cannot prove, and where the system falls short — including the one fact that matters most: an image with no credentials is not evidence of anything. It is a companion to our guide on how to spot AI-generated images — that guide teaches the manual checks, and this one teaches the provenance check. And it sits alongside our comparison of AI image detection tools compared, which covers classifiers and forensic tools; this guide covers only the credential check, and it does not repeat those tools.

1. What C2PA and Content Credentials Actually Are

Start with the two names, because they are often used as if they were the same thing. C2PA is the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity — a standards body, made up of companies including Adobe, Microsoft, camera makers and news organisations, that writes the technical specification for how provenance information is recorded and attached to media. Content Credentials are the user-facing implementation of that standard, built by the Content Authenticity Initiative: the visible label, the small "cr" icon, and the record a viewer can open and read. Think of C2PA as the rulebook and Content Credentials as the label you actually see.

A Content Credential is a small, cryptographically signed record that travels with the file. It can say that a picture was captured by a particular camera, generated by a named AI tool, or edited in a named app — and it can say when, and what was done. Each edit can add a new entry, so the record becomes a short history of the file: captured, cropped, colour-graded, exported. The signature matters: if the file is changed in a way that is not recorded, the signature no longer matches, and a verification tool will say the credential is not valid. That is what "tamper-evident" means — not that the file cannot be changed, but that an unrecorded change can be detected.

Two things the credential is not. It is not a judgement about whether an image is real or fake. A credential that says "generated by an AI tool" is a fact about the file's origin; a credential that says "captured by a camera" is also a fact about origin, not a guarantee that the scene is genuine — a real camera can photograph a staged scene, a screen, or a printout. And it is not a watermark: it does not change how the image looks. It is metadata — information about the file — that lives inside the file and can be read by tools that know how to look for it.

2. Which Cameras and Apps Attach Content Credentials

Credentials are only useful if something attaches them, and the list of things that do is growing but still short. That is the honest state of the system in 2026: it exists, it works, and most images still do not carry it.

Cameras. A small number of cameras can sign a photo at the moment it is taken, recording the camera model and the capture time in a credential. Leica was first, with the M11-P, and Sony, Canon and Nikon have added credential support to some of their professional bodies through firmware updates. The number of cameras is growing, but it is still a small share of the cameras people actually use — and a phone camera, which is what most photos are taken with, usually does not attach a credential at all. If a photo was taken on an ordinary phone, do not expect to find one.

AI generators and editing apps. This is where credentials are most common today. Adobe's tools — Photoshop, Firefly and Lightroom — can attach credentials to images they generate or edit, recording the tool, the actions taken, and whether AI was used. Other AI image generators have added credential support too, and the list changes often, so check the tool's own documentation for the current state. The important point for verification is this: when a credential says an image was generated by a named AI tool, that is strong evidence, because the generator itself recorded it.

Platforms and publishers. Some news organisations and stock agencies attach or preserve credentials, and some platforms display the small Content Credentials icon on images that carry them. But most social platforms do not preserve credentials when an image is uploaded: the platform re-encodes the image, and the credential is lost. That is the central weakness of the whole system, and it is covered properly in section 5.

3. How to Check an Image's Credentials, Step by Step

Checking a credential takes about a minute, and it is free. Work through the steps in order, and always start with the best file you can get.

  1. Get the original file if you can. Credentials live in the file, and they are usually lost when an image is downloaded from a social platform, screenshotted, or re-uploaded. A copy saved from a feed is the worst file to check. Ask the person who posted it for the original, or download it from the source that first published it. If you only have a compressed copy, run the check anyway — but read a negative result as "no information", not as "no credential ever existed".
  2. Look for the Content Credentials icon. Some apps and platforms show a small "cr" mark on images that carry credentials — in a corner of the image, or in its details panel. If you see it, click it: it opens the credential record for that image. If you do not see it, that tells you nothing on its own, because most viewers do not show the icon even when a credential is present.
  3. Open the free verification tool. Go to verify.contentauthenticity.org, the free Content Credentials verification site. Upload the image file, or paste its URL if the tool accepts it. The tool reads the file and shows the credential if one is present — or says that no credential was found.
  4. Read the record carefully. A credential usually lists what made the image (camera model, app, or AI generator), when it was made, and what was done to it since — the edits, in order, with the tool used for each. Read the whole history, not just the first line: an image can be genuinely captured by a camera and then heavily edited, and both facts will be in the record.
  5. Check whether the credential is valid. The tool will tell you whether the signature matches the file. A valid signature means the file has not been changed since the credential was written, or that every change was recorded. An invalid signature means the file was changed without a record — and that is itself information: it does not prove the image is fake, but it means the credential cannot be trusted as a history of the file you are holding.

That is the whole check. It is quick, it is free, and when it finds a clear credential, it is one of the strongest single pieces of evidence you can get about an image's origin. But a clear credential is the exception, not the rule — which is why the next two sections matter more than this one.

4. What Credentials Can Prove — and What They Cannot

Credentials are powerful evidence, but only within their limits, and it is worth stating those limits plainly, because they are often overstated.

What a valid credential can prove. A valid credential can prove that a file was produced by a named tool at a named time, and that it has not been changed since — or that every change is recorded in the history. In practice, that means: a credential that says an image was generated by a named AI tool is strong evidence the image is AI-generated. A credential that says a photo was captured by a named camera, with a valid signature and no edits, is good evidence the photo came from that camera. And a credential history that shows an image was cropped and colour-graded in a named app tells you exactly what was done to it, which is more than any pixel analysis can tell you.

What a credential cannot prove. A credential cannot prove that a scene is genuine. A real camera can photograph a staged scene, a screen showing an AI image, or a printed fake — and the credential will honestly say the photo was captured by that camera, because it was. A credential cannot prove that an image is unedited in the ways that matter: an edit made outside the credential system, or an edit that strips the credential and writes a new one, will not appear in the record. And a credential cannot prove anything about an image that does not carry one — which is most images. Absence of credentials is not evidence of fakery, and it is not evidence of authenticity either. It is simply no information.

The rule to keep is simple: present credentials are evidence; absent credentials are nothing. Treat a valid credential that says "generated by AI" as strong evidence, and treat a missing credential as a reason to use the other checks, not as a verdict.

5. The Limits: Why Most Images Have No Credentials to Check

If credentials are so useful, why do most images not have them? There are four reasons, and they decide how much any credential check is worth.

Most cameras and apps do not attach them yet. The system is a few years old, and adoption is still narrow. Most phones, most cameras, and most editing apps do not write credentials. An image from any of them will have nothing to check, and that will stay true for years, because most of the world's cameras are not going to be replaced overnight.

Platforms strip them. This is the biggest limit. When you upload an image to a social platform, the platform usually re-encodes it — compresses it, resizes it, changes the format — and the credential is lost in the process. An image that had a perfectly good credential when it left the camera arrives on your feed with nothing left. Every download, screenshot and re-upload does the same thing. That is why the original file matters so much in section 3, and why a credential check on a viral image usually finds nothing: the image has been through too many hands.

Credentials can be removed deliberately. Anyone who wants to hide an image's origin can strip its credentials with the right tools, or simply screenshot it, which creates a new file with no history at all. A credential system cannot stop someone who wants to remove the record, and it was never designed to: it is a way of recording provenance for honest publishers, not a lock on dishonest ones.

A credential can be misleading. A genuine credential attached to a file that has since been edited outside the system, a credential written by a tool that was misused, or a credential that records a real camera photographing a fake scene — all of these are ways a credential can be technically valid and still not tell you what you want to know. The signature proves the file was not changed after the credential was written. It does not prove the story the image is being used to tell.

6. Using Credentials with Other Checks: A Practical Workflow

Credentials are one check among several, and they work best as the first check in a short workflow, because they are free and fast — and because a clear credential can settle the question immediately.

  1. Start with the source. Who posted the image? Is the account real and established? Run a reverse image search. If the image appeared before with a different story, you already have your answer, and no credential check is needed.
  2. Check the credential. Upload the original file, if you have it, to verify.contentauthenticity.org. A valid credential that says the image was generated by an AI tool is strong evidence. An empty result means nothing on its own — move on to the next step.
  3. Run two tools that check different things. Use one AI classifier and one forensic check, as described in our comparison of AI image detection tools compared. Two tools that agree are much more convincing than one alone.
  4. Inspect the image yourself. Look at it slowly, at full size: the hands, the teeth, the ears, any text, the lighting and the background. For the manual checks step by step, use our guide on how to spot AI-generated images.
  5. Weigh the evidence and state it honestly. Say "the credential says this was generated by an AI tool" or "there is no credential, one detector flagged it, and the source cannot be confirmed" — not "this is definitely fake" or "this is definitely real". If the evidence does not settle it, say so plainly and do not share the image as verified.

The Bottom Line

C2PA Content Credentials are the best provenance check we have: a free, tamper-evident record of where an image came from and what has happened to it, readable in a minute at verify.contentauthenticity.org. When a valid credential is present, read it — a credential that says an image was generated by a named AI tool is strong evidence, and a credential history that shows exactly what was edited is more than any pixel analysis can tell you. But most images have no credentials, because most cameras and apps do not write them and most platforms strip them, and absence proves nothing. Credentials can be removed, and a valid credential cannot prove that the scene in front of the camera was genuine. Use the credential check first, because it is fast and free, then add the source check, the detectors and your own inspection — and be honest about what the evidence shows. No single check is 100% reliable, and no honest verification method claims to be.
