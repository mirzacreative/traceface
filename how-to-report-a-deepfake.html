How to Report a Deepfake: Platforms, Evidence & Steps

November 25, 2026 · Muhammad Atif Iqbal

A video appears in which a real person seems to say or do something they never said or did. A voice message arrives in a familiar voice, asking for money. A photograph circulates that shows a person in a place they have never been. The first impulse is to argue with it, to share it with a warning, or to message the account behind it. Each of those impulses can make the harm worse, by spreading the content further, by alerting the person behind it, or by destroying the evidence that a platform or an authority needs. The calmer course, and the more effective one, is a short routine: preserve the evidence first, report the content where it appears, tell the person affected through a trusted channel, and escalate only if removal stalls or the harm continues. This guide explains that routine, step by step, for videos, images, and cloned voices, on social media platforms, on websites, and in search results. It is a reporting guide, not a detection guide. Our guides to spotting AI-generated images, deepfake detection tools, and how deepfake detection works cover how to judge whether content is manipulated, and our guide on protecting yourself from deepfake scams covers the human checks — code words, call-backs, and second channels — to use when a face or a voice is being used to build trust. This guide starts where those guides end: the content has been found, it is causing harm or it could, and it needs to be reported and, where possible, removed. No method in this guide is presented as a guarantee, and no detection or reporting step is 100% reliable. Reporting is a process, with human reviewers, changing policies, and limits, and the guide is honest about those limits throughout. It is general information only, not legal advice. The law on manipulated media differs by country, and a specific case should be discussed with a qualified lawyer or a trusted support organisation.

1. First, Decide What You Are Dealing With — and Slow Down

Before you report anything, take a minute to describe, in your own words, what the content is, who it depicts, where you saw it, and what harm it is doing or could do. That description is the core of every report you will file, and writing it down first makes every later step faster and clearer. Is the content a video, an image, an audio clip, or a combination? Does it depict a real, identifiable person, and if so, who? Does it show the person saying, doing, or appearing in a situation they did not consent to? Was it posted by the person depicted, by someone else claiming to be them, or by an anonymous account? What does the caption, the message, or the surrounding post claim, and what is it asking the viewer to believe or to do? And what is the harm: is it reputational, financial, threatening, harassing, or intimate in nature? A deepfake used to impersonate a chief executive and ask staff for money is a fraud problem. A deepfake used to place a person in an intimate image they never consented to is a consent and harassment problem. A deepfake used to make a public figure appear to endorse a product is a deception problem. The reporting category, the platform's process, and the authority to contact all depend on that description, so the description is not paperwork. It is the decision that shapes the rest of the routine.

Slowing down also means resisting three common impulses. The first is to share the content with a warning. Sharing, even with a caption that says "this is fake", spreads the file to people who would not otherwise have seen it, gives it more views, and creates new copies that will themselves need to be reported. The second is to comment on the post, or to message the account, in anger. A comment draws attention to the post, and a message alerts the person behind the account that they have been noticed, which may lead them to delete the evidence, move to a new account, or target the person who messaged them. The third is to delete the evidence from your own device, in distress or in an attempt to forget it. Deleting the evidence makes reporting harder, because a platform or an authority needs the link, the file, and the record of when and where it appeared. The right first actions are quiet ones: preserve, note, and report, in that order, without publicising the content. If the content is distressing, step away from it after preserving it, and ask a trusted person to help with the reporting steps, rather than handling the material alone. You do not need to watch a harmful video repeatedly to report it. One viewing, with the evidence preserved, is enough.

2. Preserve the Evidence Before You Report

Evidence disappears quickly online. Posts are edited, accounts are deleted, links change, and a platform may remove content before you have a record of it, leaving you with no proof that it existed. Preserve the evidence before you file the first report, and preserve it in a way that keeps it usable. Start with the link: copy the full link to the post, the video, the profile, or the page, and paste it into a note with the date and time you saw it. A link alone is not enough, because the link may stop working, so take screenshots as well. Capture the content itself, the account name and profile picture, the caption or message, the date and time shown, the number of views, likes, shares, or comments if shown, and any comments that show the harm or the claim being made. Capture the whole screen, not only the video, so that the context is visible, and take more than one screenshot if the content is long or if the caption matters. If the content is a video, note its length, and capture a still frame that shows the key moment, without re-uploading the video yourself.

If you have the original file — a video, image, or audio file sent to you, or downloaded by you — keep an unedited copy in a folder, and do not edit, crop, compress, rename in a way that loses information, or re-save it before reporting. The original file may carry metadata, such as the date, the device, or the software used, and editing the file can remove that information. Our guide to verifying a photo's origin with reverse image search explains the metadata and provenance checks that can be run on an original file, and our guide to C2PA Content Credentials explains the provenance information some cameras and tools now attach. Keep the original file safe, work from a copy if you need to, and do not upload a private, sensitive, or intimate file to a search engine or a detection tool without considering the privacy implications, as the reverse image search guide explains. Note, in your own words, what you saw and when, who shared the content, who else saw it, and what happened next, in a dated note written at the time, rather than from memory days later. A contemporaneous note, with dates, is more credible to a platform or an authority than a reconstruction, and it is easier to write while the details are fresh.

Keep everything in one folder, with a clear name, and keep a log. The folder might contain the link, the screenshots, the original file if you have it, the dated note, and any related messages, emails, or payment requests. The log is a simple document, with one line per action: the date, what was reported, where, the reference or case number given, and the response received. The log will matter later, when a platform asks for more information, when a second copy appears, or when an authority asks for a timeline. It will also matter to you, because reporting a deepfake can take days or weeks, and it is easy to lose track of which copy was reported where, and what was said. A folder and a log, started in the first ten minutes, are the foundation of the whole routine, and they cost nothing. Do not share the folder publicly, do not post its contents to draw attention to the case, and do not send the original file to people who do not need it. Evidence kept privately, and shared only with the platform, the host, or the authority that needs it, is evidence that stays useful.

3. Do Not Engage, Do Not Share, Do Not Pay

Do not engage with the account behind the deepfake, do not share the content, and do not pay money or share personal information in response to it. Engaging — commenting, arguing, messaging, or challenging the account — rarely leads to removal, and it can lead to the account targeting you, to the content being reposted with your comment attached, or to the evidence being deleted. Sharing — reposting, forwarding, or quoting the content, even with a warning — spreads the file, creates new copies, and increases the harm to the person depicted, who may see the content again and again through other people's warnings. If you need to warn someone, warn them privately, in words, without the file, and through a channel you already trust: "A fake video of you is circulating on this platform, posted by this account, on this date. I have preserved the evidence and I am reporting it. I have not shared it." That message warns the person, preserves the evidence, and does not spread the content.

If the deepfake is being used to ask for money, for login details, or for personal information, do not comply, and do not negotiate. A voice or video that sounds or looks like a boss, a family member, or a colleague, asking for an urgent payment, a gift card, or a password, should be verified through a second channel you already trust, before any action is taken, as our deepfake scam protection guide explains in detail. Call the person back on a number you already have, use a family code word agreed in advance, or meet in person, and do not use a number, link, or contact detail given in the suspicious message itself. If money has already been sent, contact your bank or payment provider promptly, keep the evidence, and report the fraud to the relevant authority in your country. If the deepfake is being used to threaten or blackmail you — "pay, or this video will be sent to your contacts" — do not pay, do not engage, preserve the evidence, and contact the police and a trusted support organisation promptly. Paying rarely ends a blackmail attempt, and it marks the victim as someone who will pay. The reporting steps in this guide, and the support organisations in the later sections, are the safer course.

4. Tell the Person Depicted, Through a Trusted Channel

If the deepfake depicts someone other than you, and that person does not yet know about it, tell them, privately and promptly, through a channel you already trust. The person depicted is the person most affected, and they may need to make decisions — about reporting, about legal advice, about telling an employer or a school — that only they can make. A private message, in words, without the file, is the kindest and safest way to tell them. Say what you saw, where, and when, say that you have preserved the evidence and that you have not shared the content, and ask what they would like you to do. Offer to help with the reporting steps, or to pass the evidence to them, and respect their wishes about who else is told. Some people want the content reported at once. Some people want time to seek advice first. Some people want the matter handled privately, without a public discussion. Respecting their wishes is part of reducing the harm, and it is their decision, not the reporter's, how the case is handled.

If the person depicted is a child, or a vulnerable adult, the duty of care is higher, and the reporting route is different. Do not share the content, do not show it to others, and do not attempt to investigate it yourself beyond preserving the evidence and reporting it. Contact a parent, guardian, or trusted adult where appropriate, and report the content to the platform and to the relevant child safety or law enforcement authority in your country, promptly. If the content is intimate in nature and involves a child, report it to the platform and to the authority, and do not keep, share, or view the material more than is necessary to report it. The guidance in this section is general information, not legal advice, and a specific case involving a child should be discussed with a qualified professional or a trusted support organisation as soon as possible. The principle is simple: protect the person, preserve the evidence, and let the platform and the authority, not a public discussion, handle the removal.

5. Report to the Platform Where the Content Appears

Report the content on the platform where it appears, using the platform's own reporting option, which is usually found in the menu beside, below, or behind the post, video, profile, or message. The exact labels differ by platform and change over time, so this guide describes the process in general terms rather than quoting any platform's current wording, which may have changed by the time you read it. Look for the reporting or flagging option on the content itself, not only on the account, because a report on the content asks for the removal of that content, while a report on the account asks for action against the account, and the two may follow different processes. Choose the category that best fits the harm: impersonation where the content pretends to be a real person, harassment or bullying where it targets a person, false or misleading information where it deceives viewers, and non-consensual intimate content where that category is offered and the content is intimate in nature. If no category fits exactly, choose the closest one, and use the free-text description to explain the case in plain words. A report in the wrong category may be rejected or delayed, so the category choice matters, and the description should make the case even if the category is imperfect.

In the description, state the facts, in order, without exaggeration and without speculation about who made the content. Say what the content shows, who is depicted, why you believe it is manipulated, and what harm it is causing or could cause. Mention any evidence that supports the claim that the content is manipulated, such as a mismatch with the person's known voice or appearance, a contradiction with a verified source, or a technical check you ran, while being honest about the limits of that check. No detection method is 100% reliable, and a report that claims certainty a method cannot provide will be less credible than a report that states the evidence plainly and lets the reviewer judge. Include the link, the account name, the date and time, and any related context, such as a message asking for money, a threat, or a pattern of reposting. If the platform allows an attachment, attach a screenshot, not the original file, unless the platform specifically asks for the file. Keep the description short and factual, because a reviewer may read many reports, and a clear, short report is more likely to be acted on than a long, emotional one. Keep the reference or case number the platform gives you, note the date and time of the report in your log, and check the platform's decision when it arrives, through the same report, rather than filing a duplicate report for the same content.

Different kinds of platform need slightly different handling. On a social media platform, report the post, the account, and any reposts separately, because each copy is a separate piece of content, and a removal of the original does not remove the copies. On a video platform, report the video itself, and check whether the same video has been uploaded by other accounts, under other titles, and report each copy. On a messaging app, report the message or the account through the app's reporting option, and block the account, so that it cannot contact you again, while keeping the evidence you preserved. On a forum or a community site, report the post through the site's moderation process, and contact a moderator if the reporting option is unclear, with the evidence and the link. In each case, do not file the same report repeatedly for the same content, because duplicates can slow the process, and do not file reports on behalf of the person depicted without their knowledge, unless you are reporting a clear harm and you have told them, as the previous section explains. One clear report, per copy, with the evidence, is the standard to aim for.

6. Report Impersonation, and Ask the Real Person to Report Too

Impersonation reports are often stronger when they come from the person being impersonated, or when they are supported by that person, because the platform needs to establish who the real person is. If you are reporting a deepfake that impersonates someone else, tell that person, as above, and ask them to file their own report, or to confirm yours, through the platform's impersonation process, which commonly asks for proof of identity. The proof requested varies by platform, and it may include an identity document, a link to a verified account, or a photograph, and the person should follow the platform's instructions for providing it securely, rather than posting identity documents publicly. If you are the person being impersonated, file the impersonation report yourself, with the evidence you preserved, and keep the reference number. State, in plain words, that you are the person depicted, that you did not create or consent to the content, and what the content shows you supposedly saying or doing. Do not share identity documents with anyone other than the platform, through its secure process, and do not send them to an account that claims to be the platform's support team in a message, because support scams are a common follow-on from impersonation cases.

Impersonation can take several forms, and the reporting category should match the form. A fake account using a real person's name and photograph, posting deepfake videos, is an impersonation case. A real account that has been taken over and used to post deepfake content is a hacked-account case, and the real owner should use the platform's account-recovery process, as well as reporting the content. A deepfake video posted by an unrelated account, without claiming to be the person, is a false-information or harassment case, depending on the content, rather than an impersonation case, and the category should reflect that. A voice clone used in a phone call or a voice message, with no post to report, cannot be reported to a platform in the same way, and the response is the human verification routine in our scam protection guide, plus a report to the relevant fraud or harassment authority if money, threats, or personal information are involved. Naming the form correctly, in the report, helps the reviewer to apply the right process, and it avoids a rejection that comes from a category mismatch rather than from the merits of the case.

7. Non-Consensual Intimate Deepfakes: Act Quickly, and Seek Support

Non-consensual intimate deepfakes — images or videos that place a real person in an intimate situation they never consented to — are a distinct and serious harm, and they deserve a distinct and prompt response. If you are the person depicted, or if you are helping the person depicted, act quickly, preserve the evidence as above, report the content to the platform using the non-consensual intimate content category where it is offered, or the closest harassment or privacy category where it is not, and seek support from a trusted organisation that specialises in this harm. Do not view, share, or keep the material more than is necessary to report it, do not engage with the account, and do not pay if the material is being used for blackmail. The platform's process for this category is commonly faster and more protective than its general process, because the harm is recognised as severe, but the speed and the outcome still vary by platform, and the content may need to be reported in several places, as the next sections explain. Keep the reference numbers, keep the log, and check for reposts, because intimate deepfakes are commonly reposted under new links and new accounts.

Support matters as much as removal. A person targeted by an intimate deepfake may feel shame, fear, or distress, and may be reluctant to report, to tell others, or to seek help. If you are supporting that person, believe them, do not ask to see the material, do not repeat the content, and help with the practical steps — preserving evidence, filing reports, and contacting a support organisation — at their pace. Trusted support organisations, in many countries, offer confidential advice, help with reporting and removal, and emotional support, and a lawyer can advise on the legal position, which differs by country and is changing as new laws on manipulated intimate media are introduced. This guide does not state the law for any country, and it does not promise a legal outcome. It states the practical steps: preserve, report, seek support, and escalate if removal stalls. If the person depicted is a child, follow the child-safety guidance in section 4, and report promptly to the platform and to the relevant authority, without sharing the material.

8. When the Platform Does Not Act: Appeal, Re-Report, and Escalate

Platforms do not always act on the first report. A report may be rejected, may receive no response, or may result in the content staying up, for reasons that include a category mismatch, insufficient information, a reviewer error, or a policy that does not cover the case as described. If a report is rejected, read the platform's reason carefully, and consider whether the category, the description, or the evidence can be improved. An appeal, where the platform offers one, should add information, not repeat the same report: a clearer description of the harm, a stronger piece of evidence, a link to a verified source that contradicts the deepfake, or a statement from the person depicted. If the platform asks for more information, provide it promptly, through the same report, so that the case stays together. If the content has changed — a new caption, a new account, a re-upload — report the new version as a new copy, with the new link, and note the connection to the original in the description. Do not file duplicate reports for the same unchanged content, because duplicates can be merged or ignored, and they do not speed the process.

Escalation, within the platform, means using the platform's own further processes: an appeal, a second report with better evidence, a report from the person depicted, or a contact through the platform's help centre or trusted-flagger route where one is available. Escalation outside the platform, to the host, the search engine, or an authority, is covered in the next sections, and it should be considered when the platform has not acted within a reasonable time, when the content is causing ongoing harm, or when the platform has no process for the case. Keep the log up to date through every escalation, with dates and reference numbers, because a timeline that shows a prompt first report, a clear appeal, and a reasonable wait is more persuasive to a host or an authority than a list of repeated reports with no record. And keep the expectations honest: a platform may take days or weeks to decide, a decision may be appealed by the uploader, and a removal from one platform does not remove the content from the internet. The routine in this guide is a process of reduction — fewer copies, fewer views, less harm — not a promise of instant or total removal.

9. Report to the Website Host, and to Search Engines

If a deepfake appears on a standalone website, rather than on a social media platform, report it to the website's host, and to the platform or service that runs the site, using the abuse or reporting contact the host publishes. The host is the company that provides the server or the hosting service for the site, and its abuse contact is commonly listed in the site's domain or hosting records, or on the host's own website. A report to a host should state the link, the nature of the content, the harm, and the evidence, in the same factual terms as a platform report, and should ask for the content to be removed or for the site to be reviewed under the host's terms of service. Hosts vary in how quickly they act, and some hosts will refer the report back to the site owner, so a host report is a complement to, not a replacement for, a report to the site itself where a reporting option exists. Keep the reference number and the date, and follow up once if there is no response within a reasonable time, with the original report attached, rather than filing a new report from scratch.

Search engines are a second route to reducing the visibility of a deepfake, even when the content itself remains on a site. A search engine's removal process, where it offers one, commonly allows a request to remove a link from search results for reasons including non-consensual intimate content, impersonation, or personal information, and the process is described in general terms on the search engine's own help pages. A search removal, if granted, removes the link from search results, not the content from the site, so it should be used alongside, not instead of, a report to the platform or host. When filing a search removal request, provide the link, the search terms that lead to it, the reason, and the evidence, and keep the reference number. Check the search results again after a few days, using the same terms, and report any new link that appears. Search removal is a useful tool for reducing discovery, especially for a person's name, but it does not remove the content, and it should not be presented, to the person depicted, as a guarantee that the content will never be found again.

10. Contact Authorities, and Get Legal Advice Where Needed

Contact the police or the relevant reporting authority in your country when the deepfake involves a threat, blackmail, fraud, impersonation for financial gain, harassment, stalking, or intimate content shared without consent, when a child is involved, or when the harm is ongoing and platform reporting has not stopped it. This guide is general information, not legal advice, and the law, the authority, and the process differ by country, so the first step is to find the correct authority for the place where you live, from an official source, and to ask a qualified lawyer or a trusted support organisation about the position in your case. Take the evidence folder and the log, including links, screenshots, dates, messages, and reference numbers, and present the timeline in order, without editing or deleting material before you have reported it. An authority may ask for the original file, the account details, and the record of reports already filed, and a complete, dated record will help the authority to act. If you feel unsafe, if you have received threats, or if the deepfake is being used to extort you, contact the police promptly, and do not wait for a platform decision.

Legal advice is worth seeking when the harm is serious, when the content is being used commercially, when an employer, a school, or a public reputation is involved, or when a platform or host has refused removal and a further step is needed. A lawyer can advise on the laws that may apply — which may include laws on harassment, defamation, privacy, fraud, impersonation, or the specific laws on manipulated intimate media that have been introduced in some countries — and on the remedies available, which may include a formal notice to a platform or host, a court order, or a complaint to a regulator. Legal action can be slow and costly, and it is not the right step for every case, and a lawyer's advice on whether it is worthwhile, in the specific circumstances, is more valuable than a general guide's suggestion. Do not send a legal threat yourself, do not claim a legal right you are unsure of, and do not publish the content in the course of complaining about it. A calm, factual, evidenced report, followed by professional advice where needed, is the course that best protects the person depicted and best preserves the options for removal.

11. Protect Accounts, and Prevent the Next Impersonation

After reporting, take the account-protection steps that reduce the chance of a repeat. If an account was taken over, or if a deepfake was used to obtain login details, change the password, enable two-factor authentication, and review the account's sessions, recovery details, and connected apps, through the account's own security settings. If a voice clone or a video was used to impersonate you in a call or a message, warn the people who might be contacted next — family, colleagues, or clients — privately, in words, without the file, and agree a code word or a verification routine for future requests, as our scam protection guide explains. Review the privacy settings on social media accounts, limit who can see and download photographs and videos, and think before posting high-resolution portraits, voice recordings, or videos that name family members, workplaces, or daily routines, because that material can be used to build a convincing impersonation. That advice is not a guarantee — material already published cannot be unpublished, and a determined impersonator can find material from many sources — but it reduces the easy supply of high-quality material, and it makes the next impersonation a little harder.

For public figures, creators, and businesses, a further step is worth considering: a public, verifiable channel through which genuine statements are made, and a clear statement, on that channel, that requests for money, personal information, or urgent action will never be made by video or voice message alone. A verified account, a consistent style, and a stated verification routine give followers and colleagues a way to check a suspicious message, and they give the person depicted a place to post a correction that can be found. A correction should be factual and brief, should not repost the deepfake, and should point to the reporting steps already taken. Do not debate the deepfake publicly, do not share it to "prove" it is fake, and do not name the account behind it in a public post, because publicity, even critical publicity, spreads the content and may expose the person depicted to further harm. A private report, a public correction without the file, and a verification routine for the future are the three parts of the prevention plan, and they work together.

12. The Reporting Checklist, and What to Expect Next

Use the checklist below, in order, whenever a deepfake needs to be reported. The routine takes time, and it is worth doing in full, rather than stopping after the first report. 1. Note the facts. What the content shows, who it depicts, where you saw it, when, and what harm it is doing. 2. Preserve the evidence. Link, screenshots, original file unedited, dated note, related messages, all in one folder. 3. Do not share or engage. No reposting, no commenting, no messaging the account, no payment. 4. Tell the person depicted, privately, through a trusted channel, and ask what they would like you to do. 5. Report to the platform, on the content itself, in the best-fitting category, with a short factual description and the evidence. Keep the reference number. 6. Report each copy, on each platform, and ask the person depicted to report the impersonation themselves. 7. Appeal or re-report with better evidence if the first report is rejected, and follow up through the same report. 8. Report to the host if the content is on a standalone website, and consider a search removal request. 9. Contact the police or the relevant authority, and seek legal advice, when the case involves threats, fraud, harassment, intimate content, or a child. 10. Protect accounts, warn contacts privately, and agree a verification routine. 11. Keep the log, check for reposts after a few days, and report new copies with the same evidence. 12. Seek support, for yourself and for the person depicted, from a trusted person or a support organisation, because reporting a harmful deepfake is distressing work, and support is part of the routine, not an extra.

What to expect next is a process, not an instant result. A platform may acknowledge the report at once, and decide days or weeks later. A host may ask for more information. An authority may take a statement and investigate. A search removal may take effect gradually, as results are updated. The content may reappear, under a new link or a new account, and each new copy will need a new report. That cycle is frustrating, and it is the honest picture. The measures that help are the ones in this guide: a complete evidence folder, a clear log, a prompt first report, a follow-up through the same report, and a check for reposts. Over time, the copies become fewer, the views become fewer, and the harm becomes smaller, even if no single step removes the content everywhere at once. If the process stalls, or if the harm is severe, seek professional advice, from a lawyer or a support organisation, rather than repeating the same report and hoping for a different result. And if you are the person depicted, remember that the deepfake is not a reflection of you, that reporting it is a reasonable and dignified response, and that support is available. The content was made without your consent, and the effort to remove it is the work of the platforms, the hosts, and the authorities, with your evidence and your report as the starting point.

The Bottom Line

To report a deepfake, preserve the evidence first, report the content where it appears, tell the person depicted through a trusted channel, report each copy, appeal with better evidence if the first report fails, escalate to the host and to search removal where needed, and contact the police or the relevant authority, with legal advice, when the case involves threats, fraud, harassment, intimate content, or a child. Do not share the content, do not engage with the account, and do not pay in response to a threat or a request for money. No detection method and no reporting process is 100% reliable, and complete removal from the whole internet cannot be guaranteed, but prompt, evidenced, and persistent reporting, across every place the content appears, is the most effective course available, and it reduces the harm, copy by copy. Frequently asked questions

How do I report a deepfake video on a social media platform?

Use the reporting option on the post, video, or account itself, usually found in the menu beside or below the content, and choose the category that best fits what you are reporting, such as impersonation, harassment, false information, or non-consensual intimate content where that category is offered. Describe, in plain words, what the content shows, who is depicted, why you believe it is manipulated, and what harm it is causing or could cause, and attach or link the evidence you preserved. Keep the reference or case number the platform gives you, note the date and time, and follow up through the same report if the platform asks for more information. Do not repost the deepfake to draw attention to it, because reposting spreads the harm you are trying to stop.

What evidence should I keep before reporting a deepfake?

Keep the link to the post or page, screenshots showing the content, the account name, the date and time it was posted, and any caption, comments, or messages that came with it, saved in a folder with the dates noted. If you have the original file, keep an unedited copy and do not edit, crop, or re-save it before reporting, because editing can remove information that helps a platform or an authority to check it. Note, in your own words, what you saw and when, who shared it, and who else saw it, and keep any related messages, emails, or payment requests. A clear, dated record is more useful to a platform or an authority than a long description written from memory days later.

Can a deepfake be removed from the internet completely?

Complete removal from the whole internet cannot be guaranteed, because a file that has been downloaded and re-uploaded may appear in many places, including places that are hard to reach. What can usually be achieved is removal from the main places where it is being seen: the platform where it was posted, the search results that lead to it, and the host of a website that carries it, by reporting to each in turn with the evidence you kept. Act quickly, report to every place where the content appears, keep a log of each report and each response, and check again after a few days to see whether the content has reappeared under a new link or a new account. If it reappears, report the new copy with the same evidence, and consider the escalation steps in this guide.

Should I contact the police about a deepfake?

Consider contacting the police or the relevant reporting authority in your country when the deepfake involves a threat, blackmail, fraud, impersonation for financial gain, harassment, or intimate content shared without consent, or when a child is involved, and do so promptly rather than waiting to see whether the platform acts. This guide is general information, not legal advice, and the law differs by country, so ask a qualified lawyer or a trusted support organisation about the position where you live. Take the evidence you preserved, including links, screenshots, dates, and messages, and do not delete the original material before you have reported it. If you feel unsafe, or if the deepfake is being used to threaten or extort you, contact the police and a trusted support organisation, and do not engage with the person behind the account.
