AI-Generated Product Photos: How Shoppers Can Spot Fakes

November 27, 2026 · Muhammad Atif Iqbal

The listing looks perfect. The jacket is photographed in warm, golden light, on a model whose pose is relaxed and natural. The fabric looks soft, the stitching looks even, the colour looks rich, and the price is lower than the same kind of jacket elsewhere — not impossibly low, just low enough to feel like a good find. The shop has a name that sounds established, the description is fluent, and the reviews are positive, if a little vague. The shopper orders. What arrives, weeks later, is a jacket of a different colour, a thinner fabric, and stitching that comes undone at the first wear — or nothing arrives at all, and the shop has vanished. The photograph was not a photograph of the jacket that was sold. It was an image made to sell a jacket, by software that can make a plausible image of almost anything, without ever having seen the thing it depicts. This guide explains how a shopper can check a product photo before buying, what each check can and cannot prove, and what to do if a purchase has already been made. No method in this guide is presented as 100% reliable, and no check is presented as a verdict. The guide is general information only, not legal advice. Our guide to spotting AI-generated images covers the image itself in general; our guide to spotting AI-generated profile pictures covers the faces behind accounts; our guide on C2PA Content Credentials covers provenance; and our guide on deepfake scam protection covers the case in which an image or a voice is used to ask for money. This guide starts where those guides leave off: a shopper, looking at a listing, deciding whether to buy.

1. Why Product Photos Are a Natural Target

A product photo is a promise, and a promise is easier to make with generated imagery than with photography, because photography requires the thing to exist, in front of a camera, in a place, with light, while a generated image requires only a description. A seller who does not hold the stock, who has not seen the product, or who is selling a cheaper version of the product shown can produce a convincing image in minutes, at no cost, and can produce as many variations as the listing needs: the same jacket in six colours, the same lamp in four rooms, the same chair from six angles, each image consistent enough to pass a quick look and different enough to look like a real set of photographs. The shopper, meanwhile, is looking at the images on a small screen, quickly, alongside dozens of other listings, and is judging by impression rather than by inspection. That combination — a cheap way to make a promise, and a quick way to judge it — is why product photos are a natural target, and why the checks in this guide begin before the image is examined at all, with the listing and the seller, because the context in which an image appears often tells the shopper more than the image itself.

It is worth stating plainly what a generated product photo is not. It is not always a scam, and it is not always a lie. A legitimate seller may use a generated image as an illustration, a mock-up, or a visualisation of a product that is made to order, customised, or not yet manufactured, and may label it as such. A designer may show a generated rendering of a piece of furniture that will be built to the design. A seller of a printed product may show a generated mock-up of the print on a garment that has not yet been printed. In those cases, the image is a representation, not a photograph, and the shopper who understands that is not misled. The problem arises when a generated image is presented, without any label or qualification, as a photograph of the exact product that will arrive, and when the product that arrives differs from the image in material, colour, size, or quality, or does not arrive at all. The checks in this guide are therefore not checks for "AI" in the abstract. They are checks for the gap between the image and the thing being sold, and for the honesty of the listing that presents the image. A generated image, honestly labelled as an illustration, and a genuine photograph, dishonestly presented as a photograph of a different product, are different problems, and the shopper's response should differ accordingly.

The second point to state plainly is that the shopper's position has changed. A few years ago, a photograph in a listing was, in practice, evidence that the product existed and looked as shown, because making a false photograph required skill, time, and equipment. Today, a convincing image can be made by anyone, and the photograph's evidential value has fallen accordingly. That does not mean every listing photograph is false, and it does not mean shopping online has become impossible. It means the shopper needs more than one kind of evidence before buying, particularly for a purchase from an unfamiliar seller, at a price that is notably lower than elsewhere, or for a product whose quality cannot be judged from a description alone. The checks in this guide supply that additional evidence, in an order that reflects their value: the listing first, the image second, the seller third, and the technical checks — reverse-image search and provenance — last, as supporting evidence rather than as the main event. A shopper who follows that order will catch most of the listings that deserve caution, and will do so before money has changed hands, which is the only point at which the checks are fully useful.

2. Start With the Listing, Not the Image

Before examining the photograph, read the listing as a whole, because the listing is the document the shopper is actually relying on, and because inconsistencies in the listing are often easier to spot than inconsistencies in an image. Read the title, the description, the price, the delivery information, and the seller's details together, and ask whether they agree with each other. A title that names a well-known brand, a description that does not mention the brand again, and a price far below the brand's usual price is a listing whose parts do not agree. A description that is fluent and detailed about the product's appearance, but vague or silent about its material, its size, its weight, or its contents, is a listing that describes the image rather than the thing. A delivery estimate that promises arrival in a few days, from a seller whose location, as stated, would make that impossible, is a listing whose logistics do not agree with its geography. None of these inconsistencies, on its own, proves the image is generated or the listing is false. Together, they are a pattern, and a pattern is a reason to check further before buying.

Price deserves a careful reading, because it is the signal shoppers notice first and interpret most confidently, and because it is also the signal most easily misread. A price that is much lower than the same product elsewhere can be a genuine bargain, a clearance, a different version of the product, or a sign that the product shown will not be the product sent. The way to judge it is to compare like with like: the same product, from sellers whose identity can be checked, with delivery and returns included, and with the product's specification — material, size, contents — stated in the same terms. A price comparison that compares a listing that states no specification with a listing that states a full specification is not a comparison. It is a guess. Also check what the price includes and excludes: delivery, taxes, and any fee charged on arrival, because a price that looks low before those additions may not look low after them, and because a seller who hides the total cost in the small print is a seller whose listing should be read with care in other respects too. A price that is low, a specification that is vague, and a seller that cannot be checked is a combination that should stop a purchase until the checks in the following sections have been made.

Reviews deserve the same careful reading, and the same caution about treating them as evidence. Reviews on a listing can be genuine, bought, copied, or generated, and a shopper cannot reliably tell which from the listing alone. What a shopper can do is read the reviews for the pattern they show: reviews that are all short, all positive, all written in a similar style, all posted within a short period, or all vague about the product itself — praising the seller, the speed, or the packaging, without describing the product — are reviews that should not be relied on. Reviews that describe the product in specific terms, that mention a detail a buyer would only know from receiving it, that include photographs taken by the buyer, and that are spread over time, are reviews that carry more weight, though even they can be faked, and a single detailed review should not outweigh the other checks in this guide. Check, where the platform allows it, whether the reviews are from verified purchases, and whether the seller's reviews across different products repeat the same wording, which suggests the reviews were written for the seller rather than for the product. A listing whose reviews cannot be checked, or whose reviews show the pattern above, is a listing whose image should be checked with particular care.

3. Examine the Image Itself: Where Generated Images Most Often Fail

With the listing read, examine the image, slowly, at the largest size the listing allows, because the details that betray a generated image are small, and a small image on a phone screen hides them. The examination should be systematic, working through the parts of the image in order, rather than a general impression of whether the image "looks real". Start with any text in the image: the text on a label, on packaging, on a sign, on a garment, or on the product itself. Generated images very often produce text that is misspelled, garbled, repeated, or inconsistent — letters that do not form words, words that are spelled differently in two places, a brand name that changes spelling between the front and the side of a package, or a label whose text dissolves into shapes that resemble letters without being letters. Genuine photographs can also contain blurry or partially hidden text, particularly at low resolution, so garbled text is not proof on its own. But text that is clearly wrong, in a place where a real product's text would be printed clearly, is a strong reason to check further, and text that is wrong in several places in the same image is a stronger reason still. Enlarge the image, look at the text, and read it, rather than glancing at it.

Next, examine the parts of the image that involve people, hands, and bodies, where a product is shown being held, worn, or used. Hands and fingers are a well-known weakness of generated imagery: too many fingers, fingers of the wrong length or shape, a hand that merges with the product, or a grip that does not match the way a hand would hold the thing. Faces, where a model is shown, can show the same kind of error: eyes that do not match, ears that are misplaced, teeth that are irregular, or a face that looks smooth and featureless in a way a real face does not. Clothing worn by a model can show errors of its own: a seam that does not continue, a pattern that changes or repeats unnaturally, a zip or a button that is not where it should be, or a garment that drapes in a way fabric does not drape. None of these details, on its own, proves the image is generated, because genuine photographs can be retouched, compressed, or taken at an angle that makes a detail look odd. Several of them, in the same image, in different places, are a pattern, and a pattern is a reason to check the listing and the seller with extra care before buying.

Then examine the small, repeated details of the product itself: the stitching on a garment or a bag, the buttons, the zips, the screws on a piece of furniture, the pattern on a fabric, the tread on a shoe, the keys on a keyboard. Generated images often produce details that are plausible at a glance but inconsistent on inspection: stitching that changes spacing, a row of buttons in which one button differs, a pattern that repeats with a visible break, or a detail that appears on one side of the product and not on the other, where symmetry would be expected. Examine the reflections, the shadows, and the background, which are the parts of the image the generator must invent as well as the product: a shadow that falls in a different direction from the shadow of another object, a reflection in a mirror, a window, or a glossy surface that does not show the product or the room as it should, or a background that is too perfect, too blurred, or too generic to be a real place. Examine, finally, the consistency across the listing's photographs: the same product, photographed from different angles, should look the same in colour, in shape, in detail, and in material. A listing whose photographs show the same product in different colours, with different details, or in different styles — one photograph sharp and realistic, the next smooth and illustrative — is a listing whose photographs were not taken together, and may not depict the same thing at all.

4. The Seller Check: Who Is Actually Selling This?

The seller check is the check shoppers most often skip, and it is the check that most often decides whether a purchase is safe, because a generated image in the hands of a traceable, accountable seller is a disappointment waiting to happen, while the same image in the hands of a seller who cannot be traced is a loss waiting to happen. Start with the seller's identity as the platform shows it: the name, how long the seller has been on the platform, where the seller is located, and what else the seller sells. A seller whose name is a string of unrelated words, whose account is very new, whose location is vague or changes between the listing and the seller's page, or whose catalogue contains an unrelated mixture of products — electronics, clothing, furniture, and jewellery, all with the same style of perfect photograph — is a seller whose identity should be questioned. A legitimate seller commonly sells a coherent range, has a history on the platform that can be read, and gives contact details that can be checked. A seller who cannot be checked in any of those ways is a seller from whom a purchase should not be made on the strength of a photograph alone.

Check the seller beyond the platform, where that is possible, without treating any single source as conclusive. Search for the seller's name, with the product name, and read what appears: the seller's own website, if it has one, other marketplaces where the seller trades, and any reports from other shoppers, read with the caution that reports online can themselves be false, outdated, or about a different seller with a similar name. Check the seller's website, if one is linked, for the signs of a real business: a physical address, a telephone number, a returns address, and terms that state the law that governs the sale, rather than a single contact form and a generic email address. Check the domain itself, where the shopper has the means to do so: a website registered very recently, with no history, selling a wide range of products at low prices, is a website that should be treated with the same caution as a new seller on a marketplace. None of these checks proves a seller is dishonest, and a new, legitimate seller will fail several of them, which is why the seller check is not a verdict. It is a measure of how much the shopper can find out, and how much is hidden, and a purchase from a seller about whom nothing can be found out is a purchase made on trust that has not been earned.

Two seller patterns deserve particular attention, because they are common in listings that use generated images. The first is the seller who sells the same product, with the same photographs, under several different names, on several different platforms, at different prices. That pattern suggests a seller, or a group of sellers, who does not hold the stock, who is reselling an image rather than a product, and who will disappear and reappear under a new name if the listings are removed. The second is the seller whose listings all use the same style of photograph — the same lighting, the same background, the same perfect composition — across products that would, in real life, be photographed in different places, by different people, at different times. That uniformity suggests the photographs were made, not taken, and it should prompt the shopper to ask for a real photograph of the actual item, as section 6 explains, before buying. A seller who can supply a real photograph, of the actual item, taken on request, is a seller who holds the item. A seller who cannot, or who refuses, or who sends another perfect image in the same style, is a seller who has not shown that the item exists.

5. Reverse Image Search and Provenance: Useful Checks, Stated Limits

Reverse image search is a check the shopper can perform in minutes, and it is worth performing, provided its limits are understood. The method is described in detail in our guide to reverse image search, and the principle is simple: upload the image, or paste its address, into a reverse-image search service, and see where the same image, or a very similar image, has appeared before. A match to a different product, a different seller, or a stock-photo library suggests the listing is reusing an image rather than photographing the product it sells, which is a reason for caution, and a reason to ask the questions in section 6. A match to the same product, on the seller's own website, or on a manufacturer's page, supports the listing, though it does not prove the seller holds the stock. A match to a page that describes the image as AI-generated, or that shows the image in a different context — as an illustration, a mock-up, or a piece of artwork — is strong evidence that the image is not a photograph of the product, and the listing should be treated accordingly.

The limits of reverse image search must be stated as clearly as its uses, because the check is often treated as a verdict when it is only a clue. No match proves nothing. A newly generated image will not have appeared anywhere before, and a genuine photograph of a new product, taken by the seller, will not have appeared either, so the absence of a match cannot distinguish between the two. A match proves only that the image has appeared before, not that the listing is false: a legitimate seller may reuse a manufacturer's photograph, with permission, and a manufacturer may supply the same photograph to many sellers, so a match to several sellers of the same genuine product is normal. The search may also miss a match, because the image has been cropped, recoloured, flipped, or recompressed, or because the search service has not indexed the page where the image appeared. The shopper should therefore read the matching pages, rather than counting the matches: open the pages the search returns, look at what they show, and ask whether the image there is the same product, the same seller, or a different thing altogether. A reverse-image search whose results are not read is a check that has not been performed, however many matches it returned.

Provenance checks, including C2PA Content Credentials, are a newer kind of evidence, and they should be understood in the same spirit: useful where present, uninformative where absent. C2PA credentials are information that can travel with a file, recording, where it has been added, details such as the device or software that created or edited the file, and the history of edits the file has undergone. Where credentials are present and can be verified, they are supporting evidence about the file's history, and they can show, for example, that an image was created by generative software, or that it was edited after it was taken. Our guide to C2PA Content Credentials explains how to check them and what the result means. The limits are important. Credentials are not present on most images, because most devices, software, and platforms do not yet add them, and because credentials can be removed when a file is re-saved, screenshotted, cropped, or re-uploaded. An image with no credentials is therefore not necessarily fake. An image with credentials still needs the listing and seller checks, because credentials describe the file, not the thing photographed, and a file with a genuine history can still be presented, in a listing, as a photograph of a different product. Treat provenance as one piece of evidence, to be weighed with the others, and do not treat its absence, or its presence, as a conclusion.

Detection tools, including the tools compared in our guide to AI image detection tools and the detector described in our guide to how AI deepfake detection works, belong in the same category: supporting evidence, not verdicts. A detection tool analyses patterns in the image file and returns a score, a label, or a probability, and that output is a statistical judgement, not a fact about the image. A tool may flag a genuine photograph, particularly one that has been heavily edited, compressed, or upscaled, and it may clear a generated image, particularly one that has been edited, printed and re-photographed, or passed through several rounds of compression. The shopper who treats a tool's output as proof will be wrong in both directions, at different times, and will not know which. Use a detection tool, if at all, as a reason to check further, not as a reason to buy or not to buy, and prefer the checks in this guide — the listing, the seller, the reverse-image search, and the request for a real photograph — which are checks on the transaction, not on the pixels alone. The transaction is what the shopper is deciding about, and the transaction is where the evidence matters most.

6. Ask the Seller: The Check That Costs Nothing and Proves the Most

The most direct check available to a shopper is also the simplest: ask the seller, through the platform's own messaging, for a photograph of the actual item being sold, taken in a way that cannot be faked by reusing a stock image. Ask for a photograph of the item with a specific, ordinary object beside it, or with a handwritten note showing the date and the seller's name, or from an angle the listing does not show, or of a specific detail the shopper names — the label inside a garment, the underside of a piece of furniture, the contents of a box. A seller who holds the item can supply such a photograph quickly, with a phone, without preparation, and will usually do so willingly, because the request is reasonable and the sale depends on it. A seller who does not hold the item, or who is selling a different item from the one shown, cannot supply it, and will respond with delay, with excuses, with another perfect image in the same style, or with silence. The response, or the absence of a response, is itself evidence, and it is evidence about the transaction, not about the pixels.

Ask, at the same time, the questions the listing leaves unanswered, and judge the answers by their specificity. Ask what the item is made of, in exact terms, and what its measurements are, in the units the shopper uses. Ask where the item will be sent from, how long delivery will take, and what the returns process is, including who pays for a return and what happens if the item arrives different from the listing. Ask whether the photograph in the listing is a photograph of the exact item that will be sent, or an illustration, a mock-up, or a photograph of a similar item, and ask for the answer in writing, in the platform's messaging, so that it is recorded. A seller who answers specifically, in the platform's messaging, and whose answers agree with the listing, is a seller who has given the shopper something to rely on. A seller who answers vaguely, who moves the conversation off the platform to a private channel, who refuses to answer, or whose answers contradict the listing, is a seller who has given the shopper a reason not to buy. Moving the conversation off the platform is a particular warning sign, covered in our deepfake scam protection guide, because it removes the shopper from the platform's protection, its messaging record, and its dispute process, and it should be refused, whatever the reason given for it.

Keep the correspondence, whatever its outcome. If the purchase goes ahead, the messages are part of the record that supports a later claim, if the item differs from the listing. If the purchase does not go ahead, the messages are the evidence on which the decision was based, and they may be useful if the listing is reported. Do not buy from a seller whose answers have raised a doubt, in the hope that the item will prove the doubt wrong, because the doubt will have been raised by the seller's own answers, and the item, if it arrives, will arrive after the money has gone. The request for a real photograph, and the questions that accompany it, cost the shopper nothing but a few minutes, and they are the checks most likely to change the decision, because they test whether the seller holds the thing being sold, which is the question the whole purchase turns on.

7. Payment, Returns, and the Safety Net: General Information

Payment method and returns policy are the shopper's safety net, and they should be checked before the purchase, not after it, because a safety net that is discovered to be absent after the purchase is no safety net at all. Prefer, wherever possible, a payment method that is made through the platform, that leaves a record, and that offers a dispute or buyer-protection process, and prefer a listing whose returns policy is stated plainly, in the platform's own terms, rather than in the seller's own words alone. Check the returns policy for the points that matter: how long the shopper has to return the item, who pays for the return, what condition the item must be in, and what happens if the item arrives different from the listing, damaged, or not at all. A policy that is vague, that places all the cost on the shopper, or that requires the shopper to contact the seller privately, outside the platform, is a policy that should be treated as a warning, and the purchase should not proceed until the position is clear. This guide does not give legal advice, and it does not state the shopper's rights in any particular jurisdiction, which vary, and which should be checked with a reliable source where the question is specific. It states the practical point: a purchase made through a protected route, with a clear returns policy, can be undone, and a purchase made outside that route, with no clear policy, often cannot.

The same caution applies to payment methods that bypass the platform. A seller who asks to be paid by a direct bank transfer, by a gift card, by a cryptocurrency payment, or by any method that leaves no record and offers no dispute process, is a seller who is asking the shopper to give up the safety net, and the request should be refused, whatever the discount offered for doing so. A discount offered for paying outside the platform is not a saving. It is the price of the protection being given up, and the protection is worth more than the discount, particularly for a purchase whose photograph has already raised a doubt. Similarly, a seller who asks the shopper to confirm receipt, or to leave a positive review, before the item has arrived, or before the shopper has had a chance to examine it, is a seller who is asking the shopper to give up the leverage the platform gives, and the request should be refused. Confirm receipt only when the item has arrived and has been checked, and leave a review only when the review can describe the item as received, because a review left early cannot be relied on, and a confirmation given early may close the dispute process before it is needed.

8. The Decision: A Checklist Before You Pay

The checklist below gathers the guide into a sequence that can be followed for any listing that raises a doubt, and it should be followed in order, before money changes hands. A listing that passes the checklist is not proven genuine, and a listing that fails one item is not proven false, but a listing that fails several items, in different sections, is a listing that should not be bought from until the failures have been explained.

  1. Read the listing as a whole. Do the title, the description, the price, the specification, and the delivery information agree with each other? Is the specification stated in exact terms, or is it vague where it should be specific?
  2. Compare the price like with like. Is the price comparable to the same product, with the same specification, from sellers whose identity can be checked, with delivery and returns included?
  3. Read the reviews for pattern, not for praise. Are the reviews specific, spread over time, and from verified purchases, with photographs taken by buyers? Or are they short, similar, vague, and clustered in time?
  4. Examine the image at full size. Check the text, the hands and faces, the small repeated details, the reflections and shadows, and the consistency across all the listing's photographs.
  5. Check the seller. How long has the seller traded, what else does it sell, where is it located, and can anything be found out about it beyond the listing?
  6. Run a reverse-image search, and read the results. Has the image appeared before, for a different product or a different seller, or as an illustration or mock-up? Open the matching pages and look at what they show.
  7. Check provenance, where available, and understand its limits. Are C2PA credentials present and verifiable? If they are absent, do not treat the absence as a conclusion.
  8. Ask the seller, in the platform's messaging. Ask for a photograph of the actual item, from an angle or with a detail you name, and ask whether the listing photograph is a photograph of the exact item, an illustration, or a mock-up. Keep the answer.
  9. Check payment and returns before paying. Pay through the platform, by a method that leaves a record and offers a dispute process, and confirm the returns policy, including who pays for a return.
  10. Do not move off the platform, and do not confirm receipt early. Refuse any request to pay outside the platform, to confirm receipt before the item has been checked, or to leave a review before the item has arrived.

A shopper who follows the checklist will sometimes decide not to buy, and will sometimes decide to buy from a different seller, at a higher price, for the same product. That is not a failure of the checklist. It is the checklist working, because the price difference between a seller who can be checked and a seller who cannot is the price of the evidence that the product exists and will arrive as shown, and a shopper who pays the lower price to a seller who cannot be checked is buying the image, not the product. The same logic applies to the time the checklist takes: a few minutes spent checking, before a purchase, is time saved from the weeks spent pursuing a refund, reporting a listing, and waiting for a replacement that may never arrive. Shopping online, for products whose quality cannot be judged from a description, is a task that rewards slowness, and the checklist is a way of being slow in the right places, before the money has gone, rather than after it.

9. If You Have Already Bought: Preserve, Contact, Report

If a purchase has already been made, and the item that arrives differs from the listing, or does not arrive at all, the shopper's task changes from prevention to evidence, and the evidence should be preserved before anything is discarded, returned, or altered. Keep the listing as it appeared, including screenshots with the date, showing the photographs, the description, the price, and the seller's details, because a listing can be changed or removed, and the version the shopper relied on is the version that matters. Keep the order confirmation, the payment record, and any message from the seller, in the platform's messaging, and keep the item as it arrived, photographed as delivered, with its packaging, because the platform or the payment provider may ask for evidence of what was received, and the evidence is strongest when it is taken promptly and without alteration. Do not discard the item or its packaging until the process is complete, and do not alter the item in a way that prevents it being examined, because the item itself is the evidence that it differs from the listing.

Contact the seller first, through the platform's own messaging, and state plainly what arrived and how it differs from the listing, with the photographs and the order details, and ask for the resolution the platform's process provides. Keep the message factual, specific, and polite, and keep a copy of every message, because the correspondence is part of the record, and because a factual message is more likely to produce a resolution than an accusation. If the seller does not resolve the matter, or does not respond, follow the platform's dispute or resolution process, within its deadlines, and supply the evidence preserved above. If the payment was made by a method that offers buyer protection, check its process and its deadlines, and follow them, keeping copies of every submission. The deadlines matter, because a dispute raised late may be refused however strong the evidence, and the shopper should check the deadline at the outset, rather than discovering it when it has passed. This guide does not give legal advice, and it does not state the shopper's rights in any jurisdiction, which should be checked with a reliable source where the question is specific. It states the practical sequence: preserve the evidence, contact the seller in the platform's messaging, follow the platform's process, and keep a copy of everything.

Report the listing to the platform, following its reporting process, whether or not the individual purchase is resolved, because a listing that uses a false photograph to sell a product will be shown to other shoppers, and the report is the mechanism by which the platform learns of it. The report should state the facts, with the evidence, and should describe the gap between the listing and the item, or the failure to deliver, rather than making a claim about the seller's intention, which the shopper cannot know. If the listing is on a website rather than a marketplace, report it through the website's own contact or reporting process, and, where the website is a scam, consider reporting it to the relevant consumer-protection or fraud-reporting body in the shopper's jurisdiction, following that body's process. Keep a note of every report, with the date and the reference given, because the note is the record that the report was made, and it may be needed if the matter is pursued further. A report made promptly, with evidence, helps the next shopper who sees the same listing, and it is the part of the process that turns an individual disappointment into a check on the seller.

10. What Honest Sellers Can Do, and What Shoppers Should Expect

The checks in this guide place a burden on the shopper, and it is fair to ask what the burden on the seller should be, because a market in which every shopper must perform a forensic examination of every listing is a market that has failed. Honest sellers can reduce that burden, and shoppers should expect them to do so, by photographing the actual product they sell, in the condition in which it will arrive, with its real colour, its real material, and its real details, and by labelling any illustration, mock-up, or rendering as such, in words, on the listing, where the shopper will see it before buying. A seller who uses a generated image as an illustration should say so, plainly, and should show, alongside it, a real photograph of the product, or should state plainly that no real photograph exists because the product is made to order, and should describe the finished product in terms the shopper can check on arrival. A seller who photographs a sample, and sells a production version, should say so, and should state how the production version may differ. These are not onerous requirements. They are the ordinary honesty of a sale, applied to a new kind of image, and a seller who meets them will pass the checks in this guide without difficulty, because the checks are checks for honesty, not checks for perfection.

Shoppers, for their part, should expect that honesty, and should reward it, by buying from sellers who provide it, and by asking the questions in this guide where it is absent, rather than by buying on the strength of a perfect image alone. The perfect image is the image that should be questioned, because a real product, photographed in a real place, has imperfections — a crease, a shadow, a background that is not quite tidy — and an image with no imperfections at all is an image that has been made, or retouched, to a standard reality does not reach. That is not a reason to distrust every good photograph. It is a reason to judge a listing by the whole of its evidence — the listing, the seller, the price, the reviews, the reverse-image search, the provenance, and the seller's answers — rather than by the image alone. A shopper who judges by the whole evidence will sometimes pay more, will sometimes wait longer, and will sometimes decide not to buy, and will, over time, receive the products the listings promised, which is the point of shopping, and the point of the checks. Start with the listing, examine the image, check the seller, ask for a real photograph, and pay through the platform, with the returns policy checked before the money goes. This guide is general information only, not legal advice. No method in this guide can prove, on its own, that an image is real or fake, and no check should be treated as a verdict. The checks are evidence to be weighed together, with the listing and the seller, before a purchase, and the decision to buy, or not to buy, remains the shopper's judgement on that evidence.

Frequently asked questions

Can I tell from looking at a photo whether it is AI-generated?

Not with certainty, from looking alone. Some AI-generated images contain visible errors — in hands, text, reflections, or repeated patterns — and a careful look can raise suspicion. But many generated images contain no visible error, and many genuine photographs contain odd details, compression artefacts, or editing that can look like errors. Looking is a useful first check, and it is not a verdict. Combine it with the listing check, the seller check, a reverse-image search, and, where available, a provenance check, and treat a photo that passes every visual check as still unproven, not as proven genuine.

What details in a product photo should I check first?

Check the parts of the image where generated images most often fail: text on labels, packaging, and signs, which may be misspelled, garbled, or inconsistent; hands and fingers holding the product, which may have the wrong number or shape; small repeated details such as stitching, buttons, zips, and patterns, which may change or repeat unnaturally; and reflections, shadows, and backgrounds, which may not agree with the light on the product. Also check consistency across the listing's photos: the same product should look the same in every photo, in colour, shape, and detail. A single odd detail proves nothing on its own. Several odd details, in different parts of the image, are a reason to check further before buying.

Does reverse image search prove a product photo is fake?

No. Reverse image search shows where an image, or a similar image, has appeared before, and that information can be useful, but it does not prove an image is real or fake. A match to a different product, a different seller, or a stock photo suggests the listing is reusing an image rather than photographing the product it sells, which is a reason for caution. No match proves nothing, because a newly generated image will not have appeared anywhere before, and a genuine photograph of a new product will not have appeared either. Use reverse image search as one check among several, and read its results carefully: check what the matching pages actually show, rather than treating a match, or the absence of a match, as a conclusion.

What is C2PA, and does it prove a photo is real?

C2PA Content Credentials are a form of provenance information that can travel with a file, recording, where it has been added, details such as the device or software that created or edited the file. Where credentials are present and can be verified, they are useful supporting evidence about the file's history. They do not prove that the thing photographed is the thing being sold, they are not present on most images, and they can be removed when a file is re-saved, screenshotted, or re-uploaded. An image with no credentials is therefore not necessarily fake, and an image with credentials still needs the listing and seller checks in this guide. Our C2PA guide explains the check in detail.

What should I do if I already bought something shown in a fake photo?

Keep everything: the listing as it appeared, including screenshots with the date, the photos, the description, the price, and the seller's details; the order confirmation and the payment record; and the item as it arrived, photographed as delivered. Contact the seller through the platform's own messaging, state plainly what arrived and how it differs from the listing, and ask for the platform's resolution process if the seller does not resolve it. Report the listing to the platform, following its reporting process, and keep a note of the report. If you paid by a method that offers buyer protection, check its process and its deadlines, and follow them, keeping copies of every message. Do not discard the item or its packaging until the process is complete, because the platform or the payment provider may ask for evidence.
