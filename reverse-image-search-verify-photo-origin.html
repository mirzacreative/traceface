Reverse Image Search: How to Verify a Photo's Origin

November 24, 2026 · Muhammad Atif Iqbal

A photo arrives in a message, a post, or a news feed. It shows a place, a person, an event, or a product, and it asks you to believe something: that this happened, that this person is who they say they are, that this product is real, that this is the scene it claims to be. Before you share it, buy from it, or act on it, there is one check worth doing first, and it takes less than a minute. Reverse image search lets you ask the internet a simple question: where else has this image, or an image very like it, appeared, and what was it called there? The answer is often revealing. The photo of a "breaking news" event may turn out to be years old and from another country. The profile picture of a new friend may turn out to belong to a model on a stock photo site. The product photo in a too-cheap advertisement may turn out to have been copied from a legitimate shop. And sometimes the answer is nothing at all — no matches — which is itself information, although it proves far less than people assume. This guide explains how to run a reverse image search properly, on Google Lens, Bing Visual Search, and Yandex, how to read the results, what a match can and cannot prove, and which further checks to run next. No method in this guide is 100% reliable, and no single check should be treated as proof. Reverse image search is evidence. The other checks in this guide, and the guides linked throughout, are the rest of the picture. Together, they let you judge a photo with care, rather than with a guess.

1. What Reverse Image Search Is — and What It Is Not

Reverse image search works in the opposite direction from an ordinary search. In an ordinary search, you type words and get images. In a reverse image search, you give the search engine an image — by uploading a file, pasting a link, or dragging the image into the search box — and the engine returns pages where the same image, or a visually similar image, appears, along with the words those pages used to describe it. The engine does this by comparing the visual content of your image with the images it has indexed: shapes, colours, patterns, and distinctive features, rather than the file name or the text around it. That is why a reverse image search can find a photo even when it has been given a new file name, a new caption, or no caption at all, and why it can find versions of the same photo that have been cropped, resized, or lightly edited. It is also why the search has limits: a photo that has been heavily cropped, flipped, filtered, or compressed may no longer match its original closely enough for the engine to recognise it, and a photo that has never been published online will have no matches, because there is nothing in the index to match it against.

Reverse image search is not a truth detector, and it is not an authenticity check. It cannot tell you who took a photo, when it was taken, whether it was edited, or whether it was generated by AI. It can only tell you where the image, or something like it, has appeared before, and what those appearances said about it. A match on a reputable news site, from the date the event is said to have happened, supports the claimed origin. A match on a stock photo site, under a different name and a different story, undermines it. A match on a page from years earlier, in a different place, shows that the image is older than the claim. And no match shows, at most, that the search engine has no indexed copy of the image — which may mean the image is new, or private, or changed, or simply not indexed. That distinction, between what the search shows and what it proves, runs through the whole guide, because most mistakes with reverse image search are not mistakes in running the search. They are mistakes in reading the result: treating a match as proof, or treating no match as proof of the opposite. Read the result as evidence, in the same way you would read a witness statement: useful, fallible, and strongest when it agrees with other evidence.

2. Before You Search: Get the Best Copy and Note the Context

The quality of the search depends on the quality of the copy you search with, so spend a minute getting the best copy you can. If the image came as a file — an attachment, a download, a file shared in a message — use the original file, not a screenshot of it. A screenshot is a new image, made by photographing the screen, and it adds borders, interface elements, compression, and scaling that make the match harder and the metadata useless. If the image is on a web page, open the image itself — usually by right-clicking or long-pressing and choosing to open the image in a new tab — and save or copy that file, rather than screenshotting the page. If you only have a link to the image, a link can be searched directly in some tools, which avoids downloading the file at all. If the only copy you have is a small, compressed version from a social media post, search with that copy first, and then try again with a cropped version that isolates the main subject, because a search engine may match the subject more easily when the surrounding clutter is removed. Keep the original file safe, and do not edit it before searching: cropping, rotating, or filtering the only copy you have, before you have searched with it, destroys evidence you may need later, including the metadata described in section 8.

While you have the image open, note the context it came with, before the search changes your view of it. Write down, or screenshot for your own records, where you saw it, who shared it, what the caption or message said, what date and time it was posted, and what it was asking you to believe or do. Context is evidence too, and it is evidence that disappears when a post is edited or deleted. Note the account that shared it: how old is the account, what else has it posted, does it have a history that matches the claim, or does it post the same kind of image many times with different stories? Note the claim itself, in the sharer's own words, because the search will test that claim, and you need to know what you are testing. A photo shared with no claim — a profile picture, a product photo, a landscape — is tested differently from a photo shared with a specific claim — "this happened yesterday in this city", "this is me", "this is the item you will receive". The first kind of test asks where the image came from. The second kind asks whether the image matches the story. Both are reverse image search jobs, and both need the context noted before you start.

3. Google Lens: Step by Step

Google Lens is the reverse image search most people will try first, and it is built into Google Images and into the Google app on phones. On a computer, open Google Images, select the camera or Lens option in the search box, and upload the image file, paste the image link, or drag the image into the box. On a phone, open the Google app, tap the camera or Lens icon, and choose the image from your gallery, or take a photo of the image if you have no file — although a file is always better than a photograph of a screen. Lens will show visually similar images, and pages that contain the same or a similar image, along with the text from those pages. Look first at the exact matches, if Lens offers them: pages that contain the same image, rather than merely a similar one. Then look at the visually similar results, which show images that look alike — the same subject, the same scene, the same product — but are not the same file. Both are useful, for different reasons. An exact match on a stock site or an old news page is strong evidence about the image's origin. A visually similar result may show the same scene from a different angle, or the same product from a different seller, which helps you judge whether the image is a stock photo, a press photo, or a one-off picture.

Use Lens's tools to narrow the search. If the image is a photograph of a scene with a person or an object in the foreground, Lens lets you draw a box around part of the image and search for that part alone. That is useful when the whole image returns little: the background may be generic, while the person, the sign, the vehicle, or the building in the foreground is distinctive. Try the full image first, then the cropped subject, then a different crop, and note what each search returns. If Lens returns results in another language, use the page titles and the dates as a first guide, and translate the page if you need the detail: a match on a page from years ago, in any language, is still a match, and the date matters more than the language. If Lens returns no useful results, do not stop there. Lens indexes a large part of the web, but not all of it, and a different engine may have indexed the page your image came from. Try Bing and Yandex next, as described below, before concluding anything. And remember that Lens results change over time, as pages are indexed and removed, so a search run today may return different results from a search run next month. Note the date you searched, and keep a screenshot of the results for your own records, in case the pages change.

4. Bing Visual Search: Step by Step

Bing Visual Search is Microsoft's reverse image search, and it is worth running even if Google Lens has already returned results, because Bing indexes different pages and presents the results differently. On a computer, open Bing Images, select the image-search option — the camera icon in the search box — and upload the image, paste the image link, or drag the image into the box. On a phone, open the Bing app or Bing in the browser, and use the same camera option to choose the image from your gallery. Bing will return visually similar images, pages containing the image, and related searches, and it will often suggest cropped regions of the image — a face, an object, a sign — that you can search separately. The related searches are useful in a different way from the matches: they show the phrases Bing associates with the image, which can point you towards the topic, the place, or the product the image is connected with, even when the exact source page is not in the results. If Bing returns a result that looks promising but the page is in another language, open it and translate it, as with Lens, and note the date and the description the page gives the image.

Bing has a particular strength worth noting: it is often good at finding product images and shopping results. If the image you are checking is a product photo — an item for sale, a piece of clothing, a gadget, a piece of furniture — Bing's shopping results may show the same product, from the same or a different seller, with a price and a shop name. That is directly useful for a scam check: if the same product photo appears on a legitimate shop's site at one price, and on a new, unknown shop's site at a much lower price, with the same photo and a different story, the new shop's listing deserves caution. It is also useful for a provenance check in the other direction: if the product photo appears only on the shop that is selling it, and nowhere else, that is consistent with a genuine listing, although it does not prove it. As with Lens, try the full image and a cropped version, and try the image link as well as the uploaded file if you have both. Note the results, with the date, and compare them with the Lens results before you draw a conclusion. Two engines agreeing on an origin is stronger evidence than one engine alone, and two engines disagreeing is a sign to keep checking, not a sign to pick the answer you prefer.

5. Yandex Image Search: Step by Step

Yandex image search is the third engine worth trying, and it is sometimes the one that finds what the other two miss, particularly for faces and for images that have circulated widely on social media. Open Yandex Images, select the camera icon in the search box, and upload the image or paste the image link. Yandex will return similar images, pages containing the image, and — for images that contain a face — results that Yandex considers to show the same or a similar face. The face results need careful handling, and the privacy section of this guide explains why: searching with a photograph of a real person can surface other photographs of that person, from other contexts, and that information should be used with care, for verification, and not shared further. For the purpose of this guide, the face results are useful in two narrow cases: checking whether a profile picture belongs to the person claimed, or to someone else entirely, and checking whether a photograph presented as a private, one-off picture has in fact been published widely. In both cases, note what you find, and do not use the search to identify a private individual, to find personal details, or to contact someone you have no reason to contact.

Yandex's other strength is its handling of edited and re-uploaded images. An image that has been cropped, flipped, or lightly filtered may still match in Yandex when it does not match in Lens or Bing, because the engines compare images in different ways. If Lens and Bing return nothing, and Yandex returns a match, treat the match as a lead, not as a verdict, and verify it by opening the matched page, checking its date, and checking whether the image on the page is the same image, or a similar one. If Yandex returns many similar faces or many similar scenes, narrow the search by cropping to the distinctive part of the image, as with Lens, and by adding words to the search — the name of the place, the event, or the product, if you know it — to see whether the combination of image and words returns a clearer result. Yandex's interface and availability change from time to time, and some features may not be available in every country or on every device. If Yandex is unavailable to you, the Lens and Bing searches, plus the further checks in this guide, are still a thorough check. No single engine is essential, and no single engine is sufficient on its own.

6. Other Tools: TinEye and Specialist Searches

Beyond the three main engines, a few other tools are worth knowing about, each with a narrower job. TinEye is a dedicated reverse image search that reports where an image has appeared, when it first appeared in its index, and whether the image has been modified, cropped, or edited in the versions it found. Its index is smaller than Google's, so it will often return fewer results, but the results it does return are presented with a focus on the image's history, which makes it useful for the question "how old is this image?" — the question at the heart of many old-photo-presented-as-new claims. Upload the image to TinEye, or paste the image link, and look at the dates and the sources, oldest first, and at any note that a version has been modified. A TinEye result that shows the image on a page from years before the claimed event is strong evidence that the claim is wrong, whatever the sharer says. A TinEye result with no matches, on its own, proves little, for the same reasons as with the other engines: the image may be new, private, or changed.

Specialist searches fill narrower gaps. A search engine's ordinary text search, using words from the caption or the claim — the name of the place, the event, the person, or a distinctive phrase — can find the same story, with or without the image, and can show whether the story has been reported by a source you trust. A search on the social platform where the image appeared, using the platform's own search, can show whether the same image has been posted before, by other accounts, with other stories. A search for the image's file name, if the file name is descriptive, can occasionally find the original. None of these is a substitute for a reverse image search, but all of them add context, and context is what turns a match into a judgement. Our guides to AI image detection tools compared and deepfake detection tools compared cover the detector tools that analyse an image file itself, rather than searching for it online, and those tools are the next step when the search returns nothing and the question is whether the image is AI-generated. Use them as the guides describe: as evidence, with their limits stated, never as a single verdict. And use Traceface, this site's own free checker, on the original file where you have it, because it runs forensic analysis and reads the file's metadata alongside its AI checks, and it works best on the original file rather than on a screenshot or a compressed copy.

7. Reading the Results: Matches, Dates, and Context

Reading reverse image search results is a skill, and it is worth doing slowly. Start with the oldest match you can find. Open each promising result, and note the date of the page, the name of the site, and the description the page gives the image. The oldest match is often the most informative, because it shows the image's first known appearance, and the first appearance is the best guide to the image's origin. A photo claimed to show an event yesterday, whose oldest match is a news page from three years ago about a different event, is not a photo of yesterday's event, whatever the new caption says. A profile picture whose oldest match is a stock photo library, under a model's name, is not the picture of the person claimed. A product photo whose oldest match is a legitimate shop's listing is a photo copied from that shop, and the new listing should be treated with caution. Dates matter more than the number of matches: an image with a hundred recent matches, all copying the same old photo, is still an old photo, and the hundred copies do not make it new. Look for the source, not the crowd.

Next, read the context of each match, not just the image. The same image can be used honestly in one place and dishonestly in another: a genuine press photograph, reused with a false caption, is a genuine photograph with a false story. The search result shows you the image. The page shows you the story, and the story is what you are testing. Ask, for each match: does this page describe the image in the same way as the claim I am checking, or differently? Is this page a source I trust — a news organisation, an official account, a stock library, the photographer's own site — or a page I have no reason to trust, that copies images without credit? Does the page give a date, a place, a photographer, or a source that can be checked further? A match on a trusted source, with a date and a description that match the claim, supports the claim. A match on a trusted source, with a date or a description that contradict the claim, undermines it. A match on an untrusted source, with no date and no description, is weak evidence either way, and should not settle the question. And a similar image is not the same as the same image. A similar image shows the same subject, the same scene, or the same product, from a different angle or a different source. It is useful for understanding the subject, but it does not prove the origin of your image. Keep the distinction clear in your notes: same image, similar image, and no match are three different results, and they support three different conclusions.

8. What a Match Cannot Prove — and the Checks to Run Next

A reverse image search, even a successful one, cannot prove several things that people often assume it proves. It cannot prove who took the photograph, because the first page to publish an image is not necessarily the page of the photographer. It cannot prove when the photograph was taken, because the date of the first indexed page is the date of publication, not the date of capture, and a photograph can be published years after it was taken. It cannot prove that an image has not been edited, because a search engine matches the image you gave it, and an edited version may match its unedited original, or may not match at all, depending on how much was changed. And it cannot prove that an image is genuine, because a generated image can be published, indexed, and matched like any other image, and a real photograph can be given a false caption. The search shows where an image has appeared. The rest — who, when, whether edited, whether genuine — needs other checks, and this section lists them in the order to run them.

Check the file's metadata. A photograph taken with a camera or a phone often carries metadata — sometimes called EXIF data — in the file: the date and time it was taken, the camera or phone model, and sometimes the location. Metadata can be read with a file inspector, a photo app, or an online metadata viewer, and it is most useful when it is present and consistent with the claim. But metadata is easily removed — social media platforms usually strip it when an image is uploaded — and it can be edited, so its absence proves nothing, and its presence is supporting evidence, not proof. If you have the original file, check the metadata before you upload the image anywhere, and note what it says. If the metadata is absent, that is normal for a social media image, and it does not make the image suspicious on its own. Check for Content Credentials. Some cameras, phones, and editing tools now attach C2PA Content Credentials — provenance information that records how an image was made and whether AI tools were used to make or edit it. Our guide to C2PA Content Credentials and how to verify an image's origin explains how to check for credentials, what they show, and what their absence means. Credentials are useful when present, and absent from most images, especially after upload to a social platform, which usually strips them. Treat them as one more piece of evidence, not as a guarantee, and do not treat their absence as a sign of fakery.

Check the source that shared the image. An image from an official account, a news organisation, or a photographer's own portfolio carries more weight than the same image from a new, anonymous account with no history. Look at the account: its age, its other posts, whether it posts the same kind of image with different stories, whether it has been verified or has a history that matches the claim. Check the account's other posts for the same image, or for images that contradict the claim. Check the image itself, visually. Our guides to how to spot AI-generated images and how to spot AI-generated profile pictures explain the visual signs to look for — hands, text, lighting, reflections, backgrounds, and the small inconsistencies that generated images often show — and the limits of those signs, because a good generated image may show none of them clearly, and a real photograph may show several of them after compression. Visual checks are supporting evidence, never a verdict on their own. Ask the source. If the image matters — a purchase, a news story, a new contact, a request for money — ask the person who shared it for the original file, for more photographs of the same subject, or for the source of the image. A genuine source can usually provide more. A dishonest source will refuse, delay, or provide more images that also fail the checks. And if the image is part of a scam, see our guide on how to protect yourself from deepfake scams, which covers the verification routine — code words, call-backs, and second channels — that applies when a face or a voice, as well as a photograph, is being used to build trust.

9. Special Cases: Profile Pictures, News Photos, and Product Photos

Different kinds of image need different readings of the same search results. Profile pictures are the most common reverse image search job, and the question is usually identity: is this person who they say they are? A reverse search that finds the same picture on a stock site, a model's portfolio, or another person's profile, under a different name, is strong evidence that the picture does not belong to the person using it. That does not prove the person is a scammer — people reuse pictures for many reasons — but it does mean the picture cannot be used as proof of identity, and the person should be verified through another channel before trust, money, or personal information is involved. Our guide to spotting AI-generated profile pictures covers the case where the picture has no matches at all, because it was generated rather than copied, and the two guides together cover the two ways a fake profile picture is made: copied from someone else, or generated from nothing. A profile picture with no matches, on its own, is not proof of fakery, and a profile picture with matches is not proof of honesty. Read the matches, read the account, and verify the person, in that order.

News and event photos are the second common job, and the question is usually time and place: did this happen, here, now? A reverse search that finds the same photo on a page from years ago, or from a different country, answers that question directly, and the answer is no, whatever the new caption says. Old photos reused as new ones are one of the most common forms of misinformation, because a real photograph, given a false caption, feels more convincing than a invented one. Check the oldest match, check the date and the place on the original page, and check whether a trusted news source has reported the event the photo is said to show, with its own photographs. If no trusted source has reported it, and the only source is the post itself, treat the claim as unverified, however dramatic the photograph. Product photos are the third common job, and the question is usually legitimacy: is this shop selling what it shows? A reverse search that finds the same product photo on a legitimate shop's site, at a different price, is a warning sign, especially if the new shop is newly created, has no reviews, no clear contact details, and asks for payment by a method that cannot be reversed. A product photo with no matches is not proof of legitimacy, but it is consistent with a genuine listing, and it should be read alongside the shop's other signs: its age, its reviews, its contact details, and its payment methods. In all three cases, the search is the first check, not the last, and the decision should rest on the combination of the search, the source, and the context, not on any one of them alone.

10. Privacy and Safety: What Not to Upload

Reverse image search involves sending an image to a search service, and that has privacy implications that matter more than the convenience of the search. Do not upload an image that shows private information: an identity document, a passport, a driving licence, a bank card, a private address, a child's photograph, a photograph of a private person who has not consented, or any image you would not want stored, indexed, or shown to others. Search services process the image you upload, and the image may be retained under the service's own terms, which you should read before uploading a sensitive image. For a sensitive image, the safer course is to check what you can from the image itself and from its context — the metadata, the account that shared it, the claim it carries — and to report it to the platform where it appeared, rather than uploading it to a search engine. If you must search with a sensitive image, crop out the private details first, search with the cropped version, and do not upload the full image. The same caution applies to images of children: do not upload a child's photograph to a reverse image search service, and do not use a reverse image search to identify a child or to find personal details about a child. If you are concerned about a photograph of a child being misused, report it to the platform and to the relevant authority in your country, and seek advice from a trusted organisation, rather than searching with the image yourself.

There is a second privacy consideration, about the person in the photograph. A reverse image search with a photograph of a real person can surface other photographs of that person, from other contexts, and that information should be used only for the verification purpose that prompted the search, and not shared further, stored, or used to contact the person. Do not use a reverse image search to find a stranger's identity, address, workplace, or personal details, and do not use it to harass, stalk, or embarrass anyone. The legitimate uses in this guide — checking whether a profile picture belongs to the person claimed, checking whether a news photo is old, checking whether a product photo was copied — are verification uses, and they should be kept to that purpose. If the search returns personal information about a private individual, do not share it, do not act on it, and delete any record you made of it that you do not need for the verification itself. The check is a tool for protecting yourself from being misled. It is not a tool for investigating other people.

11. Limits and Reliability: Why No Result Is Final

Every method in this guide has limits, and the honest summary is that no single check, and no combination of checks, can give a 100% reliable answer about a photograph's origin. Reverse image search depends on what has been indexed, and much of the web is not indexed, or is indexed slowly, or is removed. A photograph that has never been published, a photograph published on a private account, a photograph published very recently, and a photograph that has been heavily edited may all return no matches, and none of those results proves anything about the photograph's genuineness. A photograph that has been generated by AI may return no matches because it has no original, or may return matches because it was published and copied. A real photograph that has been cropped, flipped, or filtered may return no matches because the change was enough to defeat the matching, or may return matches because the change was small. Compression, which social media platforms apply to every upload, degrades the image and the match, and a screenshot degrades it further. The search is a tool with a recall problem: it finds many things, but it misses many things too, and a miss is not evidence.

Detectors and visual checks have the same limit, in the other direction. An AI detector may call a real photograph generated, and a generated image real, and its verdict depends on the image, the tool, and the training of the model behind it. A visual check may find signs that are really compression artefacts, and may miss signs in a high-quality generated image. Metadata can be stripped or edited. Content Credentials can be stripped. A source can be faked. A caption can be copied. None of this means the checks are useless. It means they are evidence, and evidence is strongest when several independent checks agree. A photograph whose reverse search finds an old, trusted source, whose metadata matches the claim, whose credentials are present and consistent, and whose sharer is a trusted account with a history, is a photograph you can rely on with reasonable confidence. A photograph whose search finds nothing, whose metadata is absent, whose sharer is new and anonymous, and whose claim is urgent and unverified, is a photograph you should not rely on, however convincing it looks. The difference is not one check. It is the combination, and the willingness to say "unverified" when the combination does not add up. "Unverified" is a useful answer. It is the answer that stops a false photograph from being shared, a false identity from being trusted, and a false product from being bought.

12. The Verification Routine: A Checklist You Can Use Every Time

Use the routine below, in order, whenever a photograph matters — a purchase, a news claim, a new contact, a request for money or information, or any image you are about to share with others. The routine takes a few minutes, and it is the same routine whatever the image is, so it becomes a habit rather than a decision. 1. Note the context. Where did you see the image, who shared it, what did the caption or message say, and what is it asking you to believe or do? Keep a record, in case the post is edited or deleted. 2. Get the best copy. Use the original file, not a screenshot. If you only have a small or compressed copy, note that, and try a cropped version as well as the full image. 3. Search with Google Lens. Note the exact matches, the similar images, the dates, and the sources, oldest first. 4. Search with Bing Visual Search. Note the same, and compare with the Lens results. 5. Search with Yandex. Note the same, and compare with the other two. 6. Try TinEye if the question is the image's age, and note the oldest match and any modification note. 7. Read the oldest and most trusted matches. Does the date, the place, and the description match the claim, or contradict it? Note the answer in words, not just in numbers. 8. Check the metadata and any Content Credentials on the original file, if you have it, following our C2PA guide. Note what is present, and what is absent, and what each means. 9. Check the source. Look at the account or the site that shared the image, its age, its history, and whether it has a reason to be trusted. 10. Check the image visually, using our guides to spotting AI-generated images and spotting AI-generated profile pictures, and note any signs, with their limits. 11. Decide. Supported, contradicted, or unverified — and act accordingly. If the image is contradicted, do not share it, do not buy from it, and do not act on it. If it is unverified, do not share it as fact, and ask the source for more evidence. If it is supported, share it with its source, so the next person can check it too.

If the image is being used to impersonate someone — a cloned voice on a call, a fake video, a profile picture that belongs to someone else — add the human checks from our guide on deepfake scam protection: a family code word, a call back on a number you already trust, and a second channel, before money or information changes hands. If the image is a voice or a video rather than a photograph, see our guide on how to spot an AI-cloned voice and our guides to AI-generated video detection and how deepfake detection works, which cover the audio and video checks that a reverse image search cannot. And if you have the original file and want a technical check as well as a search, use Traceface, this site's own checker, which runs forensic analysis and reads metadata alongside its AI checks. The routine above, the human checks, and the technical checks together are the full verification toolkit on this site. No one tool in it is 100% reliable. Together, used in order, they are the most careful check available to an ordinary reader, and they are enough to stop most false photographs from doing harm.

13. If a Photograph of You Is Being Misused

If a reverse image search shows that a photograph of you, or a photograph you took, is being used without your permission — on a fake profile, in a scam advertisement, on a site you did not authorise — act in this order. First, keep the evidence: screenshots of the pages, the links, the dates, and the search results, saved in a folder, because pages and posts are often deleted once they are reported. Second, report the misuse to the platform where it appears, using the platform's reporting or impersonation process, and provide the evidence you kept. Most platforms have a process for impersonation and for unauthorised use of a photograph, and a report with clear evidence is more likely to be acted on than a report without it. Third, if the misuse is part of a scam — a fake profile asking for money, a fake shop using your photographs, a fake advertisement using your face — report it to the relevant fraud-reporting authority in your country, and warn the people who may be contacted next, because scammers often contact the victim's friends and family using the stolen photographs. Fourth, if the photograph is intimate, or if the misuse involves threats, harassment, or blackmail, contact the police, and seek advice from a trusted support organisation, rather than trying to resolve it by messaging the account yourself. Do not engage with the account, do not send money, and do not share personal information in an attempt to get the photograph removed. The platform's reporting process, and the authorities, are the right channels, and engaging directly with a scammer rarely helps and can make the situation worse.

To reduce future misuse, review the privacy settings on your social media accounts, and limit who can see and download your photographs and videos, as explained in our deepfake scam protection guide. Avoid posting high-resolution portraits publicly where a smaller image would do, and think before posting a photograph that names family members, your workplace, or your daily routine, because that context helps a scammer build a convincing impersonation. That advice is not a guarantee — a photograph that has already been published cannot be unpublished, and a determined scammer can find material from many sources — but it reduces the easy supply of high-quality material, and it makes misuse a little harder. If you are a photographer, consider watermarking the versions you publish for work, and keeping the unwatermarked originals private, so that a copied version can be recognised as yours. And if you find your work being used without credit, keep the original file, with its metadata, as your evidence of authorship, because the original file, with its date and its metadata, is the strongest evidence that the work is yours.

The Bottom Line

Reverse image search is the first check to run when a photograph asks you to believe something, and it is a check worth running properly: the best copy of the image, more than one search engine, the full image and a cropped version, the oldest match read first, and the context of each match read as carefully as the image itself. A match on a trusted, dated source supports a claim. A match on an old page, under a different story, contradicts it. No match proves very little, and it should never be treated as proof that an image is new, genuine, or safe. Add the further checks — metadata, Content Credentials, the source, the visual signs, and the human verification routine — and decide in words: supported, contradicted, or unverified. Do not upload private, sensitive, or children's photographs to a search service. Do not use a search to investigate a private individual. And do not treat any single check, including this one, as 100% reliable, because no check is. A photograph is evidence, and evidence needs to be read with care, in combination, and with a willingness to say "unverified" when it does not add up. Run the routine, keep the record, and share a photograph only when you can say where it came from, and why you believe the story it carries.
