Deepfake Scams: How to Protect Yourself and Family

November 23, 2026 · Muhammad Atif Iqbal

The phone rings, and the voice is your daughter's. She says she has been in an accident, she needs money urgently, and you must not tell anyone else. The voice sounds right — the same tone, the same way of speaking, the same small habits of speech. Or a video call opens, and the face on the screen is your manager, asking you to transfer funds to a new supplier account before the end of the day. The face moves, the lips move, the voice matches. In both cases, the person on the other end may not be the person you think they are. AI tools can now clone a voice from a short recording and generate a moving, speaking face from a few photographs, and scammers use both to impersonate people their targets trust. This guide explains how deepfake scams work, the forms they take, the warning signs that matter most, and — above all — the practical steps that protect you and your family: family code words, verification habits, and the pause before any payment. No detection method is 100% reliable, and this guide does not pretend otherwise. The protection that works is not spotting the fake. It is verifying the person through a second channel you already trust, every time, before money or information changes hands. It is a companion to our detection guides — how to spot AI-generated profile pictures, how AI deepfake detection works, AI-generated video detection, and how to spot an AI-cloned voice — which explain the technical checks. This guide covers the human side: what to do when the call comes.

1. What a Deepfake Scam Is — and Why It Works

A deepfake is an image, a voice, or a video that has been generated or altered by AI to look or sound like a real person. A deepfake scam uses that impersonation to commit fraud. The impersonation itself is not the whole scam. The scam is the request that follows it: money, gift cards, cryptocurrency, bank details, passwords, or access to a computer or a payment system. Scammers choose impersonation because trust is the hardest part of a fraud to build, and a familiar voice or face builds it in seconds. A stranger asking for money is easy to refuse. A daughter asking for help after an accident, a boss asking for an urgent transfer, or a colleague asking for a password reset is hard to refuse, because refusing feels like letting a real person down. That emotional pressure is deliberate. The scammer's script is designed to make you act before you think, and the impersonation is designed to remove the doubt that would normally make you pause.

Three things have made deepfake scams more common and more convincing. The first is the raw material. A few seconds of voice from a social media video, a voicemail, or a video call, and a handful of photographs from a public profile, can be enough for a voice-clone or face-swap tool to produce a passable imitation. Most people have published that much material without thinking about it. The second is the quality. A cloned voice or a generated face no longer needs to survive careful study. It needs to survive a short, stressful phone call or a brief video call on a phone screen, while the listener is worried, distracted, or under time pressure. The third is the cost and speed. Generating a voice clone or a fake video is now cheap and fast, which means a scammer can try many targets, personalise each attempt with a real name and a real relationship, and keep trying until one person pays. None of this means every unfamiliar call is a deepfake, or that every video call should be distrusted. It means that a familiar voice or face, on its own, is no longer enough proof of identity for a request involving money or sensitive information. Verification must come from somewhere else — a second channel, a code word, a call back to a known number — and it must happen before you act.

2. The Voice-Clone Call: The Most Common Deepfake Scam

The voice-clone call, sometimes called the "grandparent scam" in its older form, is the most widely reported deepfake scam, and the pattern is consistent. The call comes from an unknown number, or from a number that appears to be the relative's number. The caller sounds like a child, a grandchild, a partner, or another close relative. The story is urgent: an accident, an arrest, a hospital bill, a car breakdown, a lost wallet abroad, or a legal emergency. The caller needs money now, and asks for it in a form that is hard to reverse — a wire transfer, gift cards, cryptocurrency, or a payment app transfer. The caller asks for secrecy: do not tell Mum, do not tell Dad, do not call anyone else, there is no time. Sometimes a second voice joins the call, posing as a police officer, a lawyer, or a hospital worker, to add authority and to keep the target on the line. The whole call is designed to prevent the one action that would expose it: hanging up and calling the real person back.

The voice can be convincing, and it is a mistake to judge the call by how real it sounds. A cloned voice is built from recordings of the real person's voice, so it will sound like them, including their accent, their pace, and some of their mannerisms. It will usually be weaker on the things a recording cannot capture: the specific knowledge a family shares, the ability to answer an unexpected question, and the ability to hold a natural, unscripted conversation that goes off-script. That is why the checks in this guide focus on knowledge and on a second channel, not on listening harder for a robotic tone. A cloned voice may sound slightly flat, may pause oddly, may struggle with a long, free-flowing answer, or may repeat a phrase — and our guide on how to spot an AI-cloned voice explains those audio signs in detail. But a good clone, in a short call, may show none of them. The safest response to an urgent money request from a familiar voice on an unfamiliar number is the same whatever the voice sounds like: pause, do not send money, and verify through a channel you already trust. If the emergency is real, the real person will still need help after you have spent two minutes verifying. If it is not real, those two minutes will have saved you.

3. Video-Call Impersonation: When the Face Is on Screen

Video-call impersonation is the visual equivalent of the voice-clone call. The target receives a video call, or joins a video meeting, and sees a face they recognise: a relative, a manager, a colleague, or a client. The face speaks, nods, and reacts, and the voice matches. The request follows the same pattern as the voice call: an urgent transfer, a change of bank details, a gift-card purchase, a password reset, or access to a system. In a workplace version, the fake manager joins a meeting, or the fake colleague sends a message saying they are on a video call and need help with a payment. In a family version, the fake relative appears on a video call, looks distressed, and asks for money. The face is the proof the scammer is relying on, and it works because most people have never considered that a live video call could be faked. A video call feels like the strongest possible verification. In the age of deepfakes, it is not. It is one piece of evidence, and it needs the same second-channel check as a voice call.

There are signs to watch for on a video call, and our guides to AI-generated video detection and how deepfake detection works explain them in full: a face that does not quite track the head movement, lips that do not match the words, skin that looks too smooth, lighting that does not match the room, or a call that stays short, stays in poor quality, or avoids movement. But the same limit applies as with voice: a good video deepfake, on a small phone screen, in a short call, may show none of those signs clearly, and a real video call over a poor connection shows many of them. Poor lighting, a low-resolution camera, and a bad connection make real people look strange too. So do not rely on visual inspection alone. Instead, use a live, unscripted check: ask the person to turn their head to the side, to wave, to hold up a named object, or to answer a question you ask on the spot, about something only the real person would know. A real person can do those things naturally. A deepfake may struggle, freeze, or produce a distorted result when the face moves in an unscripted way. Even then, treat the live check as supporting evidence, not as final proof. The deciding check is the call back: hang up, call the person on a number you already have, or message them through a channel you already use, and ask whether they just called you. If the answer is no, the video call was fake, however real it looked.

4. Other Deepfake Scam Forms to Recognise

Voice and video calls are the most personal forms, but deepfake scams appear in several other shapes, and recognising the shape helps you recognise the request behind it. Celebrity endorsement scams use a generated video or audio clip of a well-known person — an actor, a presenter, a business leader — apparently endorsing an investment, a trading platform, a health product, or a giveaway. The clip usually appears as a social media advertisement or a sponsored post, with a link to a website that asks for a deposit or for personal details. The celebrity did not make the endorsement. The clip was generated, and the website is the scam. No genuine investment opportunity is announced by a celebrity in a social media advertisement asking for an urgent deposit. Romance and friendship scams use generated profile pictures and, increasingly, generated voice and video, to build a relationship over days or weeks before a request for money arrives. The profile picture checks in our guide on how to spot AI-generated profile pictures are the starting point for those cases, and the pattern to watch for is the same: a relationship that moves fast, a story that stays vague, a refusal to verify, and a request that follows the declaration of trust.

Business and supplier scams use a cloned voice or a fake video to impersonate a boss, a client, or a supplier, and to change payment details or to authorise a transfer. The request often arrives as an email first, with a follow-up call or message that appears to confirm it, so that two channels seem to agree. The two channels do not agree, because both were faked. The check is the same: contact the person through a channel you already trust, using details you already have, not details from the message that made the request. Job and recruitment scams use a fake recruiter's face and voice on a video call, or a generated video of a company executive, to lend credibility to a fake job offer, and then ask for a fee, for bank details, or for personal documents. A genuine employer does not ask a new hire to pay a fee to start work, and does not ask for bank details or identity documents over a chat message before a proper hiring process. In every form, the structure is the same: a trusted identity, an urgent request, pressure to act without checking, and a payment method that is hard to reverse. Learn the structure, and you will recognise the scam whatever face or voice it wears.

5. Family Code Words: Set Them Up Before You Need Them

A family code word is the simplest, most reliable defence against a voice-clone call, and it costs nothing to set up. It is a secret word or phrase, agreed in advance by the people in your family or household, that any family member can ask for when a caller claims to be a relative in trouble. The idea is simple: a cloned voice can copy how a person sounds, but it cannot know a secret that was never recorded, never written in a message, and never published anywhere. When the caller says "It's me, I'm in trouble, I need money", the person receiving the call asks for the code word. A real family member knows it. A scammer does not, however good the voice sounds.

Set the code word up in person, or on a call you know is genuine, and follow four rules. First, pick something hard to guess and easy to remember. A favourite meal, a childhood pet's name combined with a number, or a nonsense phrase your family uses is better than a common word, a birthday, or a name the family uses publicly. Avoid anything that appears on social media, in a family group chat, or in any message that could be read by someone else. Second, keep it secret. Do not write it in a message, do not post it, do not say it in a voicemail, and do not share it with anyone outside the family group that agreed it. A code word that has been written down in a chat is a code word a scammer may find. Third, make sure everyone who might receive the call knows it, including older relatives, who are the most common targets of voice-clone scams, and teenagers, who may receive a call impersonating a parent. Explain what the code word is for, in plain language, and practise it once, so that asking for it feels normal rather than rude. Fourth, agree what happens if the caller does not know it: hang up, call the relative back on a number you already trust, and do not send money. The code word is a check, not a password that proves everything on its own. Use it alongside the call-back check in the next section, and treat a caller who cannot give the code word, who gets angry at being asked, or who says there is no time for checks, as unverified.

Families with children should also agree a code word for collection and for messages: a word the child knows, that a stranger collecting them from school or an activity must give, and a rule that the child checks with a parent through a known number before going with anyone. The same principle applies at work. A team can agree a verification phrase or a verification step for urgent payment requests: no transfer is authorised on a single call or message, however senior the voice sounds, without a second confirmation through a known channel. The code word does not need to be complicated, and it does not need technology. It needs to be agreed before the scam call comes, kept secret, and used every time. A family that has never discussed a code word will not invent one in the middle of a frightening call. A family that agreed one last month will use it without thinking, and that is the point.

6. The Verification Steps: What to Do Before Sending Money

Verification is a short, fixed routine, and it should be the same every time a request for money, personal details, or access arrives from an unfamiliar channel, however familiar the voice or face. Follow the steps in order, and do not skip a step because the caller sounds convincing or because the request feels urgent. Urgency is the scammer's tool, and it is the reason the routine exists.

  1. Pause. Do not send money, buy gift cards, share a code, or give access while you are still on the call or reading the message. Say you need a moment, and put the phone down. A genuine emergency will survive a pause. A scam depends on you not taking one.
  2. Ask for the code word. If the caller claims to be family, ask for the family code word agreed in section 5. If they do not know it, treat the call as unverified.
  3. Ask an unscripted question. Ask something only the real person would know, and something that cannot be guessed from social media: a shared memory, a detail from a recent conversation, a question about a family event. Ask it on the spot, and listen for a specific answer, not a vague one.
  4. Hang up and call back on a known number. This is the deciding step. End the call, and call the person on a phone number you already have, saved in your phone, or message them through a channel you already use. Do not use a number the caller gave you, and do not call back the number that called you, because a scammer can spoof a number. Ask the person directly: "Did you just call me and ask for money?" If they say no, or if you cannot reach them, do not act.
  5. Check with another person. If you cannot reach the person, call another family member, a colleague, or a friend who may know where they are. A second person can often confirm, in a minute, that the relative is safe at home, or that the manager is in a meeting and made no such request.
  6. Check the payment details separately. If the request is to change bank details, pay a new account, or use a new payment link, verify those details through a separate, trusted channel — a number on a previous invoice, a contact you have used before, or the organisation's official website — never through the details in the message that made the request.

For a video call, add the live check from section 3 before the call back: ask the person to move, to hold up a named object, or to answer a question on the spot, and watch whether the image holds together. For a workplace request, add the workplace rule: no payment, no change of bank details, and no password reset is authorised on a single call or message. Every urgent request needs a second confirmation, through a known channel, from a person you have contacted yourself. Write that rule down, share it with the team, and follow it even when the request comes from the most senior person in the organisation. Scammers impersonate senior people precisely because junior staff are reluctant to question them. A verification routine that applies to everyone, with no exceptions for seniority or urgency, removes that reluctance, because checking is the rule, not a personal doubt.

7. Protecting Your Voice and Face: Reducing the Raw Material

You cannot stop a scammer from trying, but you can reduce the material available to build a clone of you, and you can make it harder for a clone to be believed. Start with your public posts. Short voice recordings — videos where you speak, voice notes shared publicly, podcast or video appearances — are the raw material for a voice clone. Photographs, especially clear, front-facing portraits, are the raw material for a face clone. You do not need to delete your online presence, and for many people that would be neither possible nor desirable. But you can make sensible choices: check the privacy settings on your social media accounts, and limit who can see and download your photos and videos. Avoid posting high-resolution portraits publicly where a smaller image would do. Think before posting a video in which you speak at length, especially one that names family members, your workplace, or your daily routine, because that recording teaches a clone both your voice and your story.

Check what your family posts about you, too, and talk about it together. A grandparent's public profile, full of family photographs and videos, is often the source material for a clone that targets the whole family. Agree, as a family, what is shared publicly and what is kept to a trusted group, and review the settings on the accounts of older relatives who may not have thought about it. The same applies to voicemail greetings: a long, chatty voicemail greeting, in your own voice, is a free voice sample. Keep the greeting short. Be careful, too, about voice and video in other contexts: a voice assistant, a customer-service call, or a video meeting can all be recorded, and you cannot control every recording. That is why reducing the raw material is a supporting measure, not the main defence. The main defence remains verification. A scammer with a perfect clone and no code word, no call back, and no second channel still fails, because the checks do not depend on how good the clone is. A scammer with a poor clone and a target who never verifies still succeeds, because the target never checked. Put your effort into the checks first, and into the raw material second.

For photographs, our guide to C2PA Content Credentials and how to verify an image's origin explains how provenance information can show where an image came from and whether AI tools were used to make or edit it. Provenance is useful when it is present, and it is absent from most images, especially after they have been uploaded to a social platform, which usually strips it. Treat provenance as one more piece of evidence, not as a guarantee. And consider a simple habit that costs nothing: watermark professional portraits you publish for work, and use a lower-resolution version for public posts. Neither step guarantees a photo will not be misused, but each one makes misuse a little harder, and together with privacy settings they reduce the easy supply of high-quality material.

8. Talking to Children and Older Relatives About Deepfakes

Children and older relatives are targeted in different ways, and the conversation with each needs to be different. Older relatives are the most common targets of voice-clone scams, because they are more likely to answer an unknown number, more likely to trust a familiar voice, and less likely to have heard of voice cloning. The conversation should be short, practical, and free of blame. Explain, in plain language, that a computer can now copy a grandchild's voice, that a call asking for money urgently may not be the grandchild, and that the safe response is always the same: hang up, and call the grandchild back on the number already saved in the phone. Agree the family code word, write the trusted numbers on a card by the phone if that helps, and reassure them that checking is not rude and that a real grandchild will understand. Repeat the conversation more than once, because a single warning, heard once, is easily forgotten under the stress of a frightening call. And agree that no older relative will ever be asked to keep a money request secret from the rest of the family: secrecy is the scammer's tool, and a family rule against secret money requests removes it.

Children and teenagers need a different conversation, because they are more likely to meet a deepfake on social media than on a phone call: a fake video of a celebrity, a fake profile using a generated face, or a video call from a stranger posing as a friend. Teach them the same verification habit in their own terms: if a message asks for money, for personal photos, for passwords, or for secrecy, stop and tell a trusted adult, however real the profile looks. Agree a family code word for collection and for messages, as in section 5, and agree that a child never goes with someone, and never shares personal information, on the basis of a call or a message alone. Keep the tone calm and practical, not frightening. The aim is not to make children afraid of every call and every profile. It is to give them one clear rule — stop, check with a trusted adult, and never act on an urgent request alone — that works whatever form the fake takes. For the profile-picture side of the problem, show them our guide on how to spot AI-generated profile pictures and talk through an example together, so the checks become familiar before they are needed.

9. If You Have Already Sent Money or Shared Information

If you realise, during or after a call, that you may have been scammed, act quickly and without shame. Scammers rely on embarrassment to delay reporting, and delay reduces the chance of recovering money or limiting harm. First, contact your bank or payment provider immediately. Explain that you believe you were scammed, give the details of the payment, and ask what can be done to stop or recover it. The sooner you report, the better the chance, although recovery is not guaranteed, and a payment made by gift card or cryptocurrency is very hard to recover. Second, secure your accounts. If you shared a password, a code, or personal details, change the passwords for the affected accounts at once, from a device you trust, and turn on two-factor authentication where it is available. If you shared identity documents, contact the relevant authority in your country for advice on protecting your identity, and watch for further attempts to use the documents. Third, keep the evidence. Save screenshots, phone numbers, links, message histories, dates, times, and payment records, and do not delete the messages or the call history. That evidence is what the bank, the platform, and the police will need.

Fourth, report it. Report the account or the advertisement to the platform where it appeared, report the phone number to your phone provider if that option is available, and report the scam to the police or the relevant fraud-reporting authority in your country. Reporting helps the authorities track patterns, even when an individual case cannot be resolved, and it may protect the next target. Fifth, warn the people the scammer may contact next. Tell family members, and colleagues if the scam was work-related, that a clone of you or of a relative is being used, and that any urgent money request in your name should be verified through a known number. Scammers often contact relatives of a victim, using the names and details learned from the first scam, and a warning in advance breaks that chain. Finally, take care of yourself. Being scammed is distressing, and it is not a sign of foolishness: deepfake scams are designed by professionals, they exploit trust and urgency, and they fool careful people. Talk to someone you trust, and do not let embarrassment stop you from reporting quickly. The fast response matters more than the mistake.

10. A Family Protection Plan: One Page, Agreed Today

Protection works best when it is agreed in advance, written down in one place, and practised once, so that it is ready when a frightening call arrives. Use the plan below as a starting point, adapt it to your family, and keep a copy where everyone can find it — on the fridge, in a family group chat, or in a shared note. Review it once a year, and whenever a new phone, a new account, or a new family member joins the household.

  1. Code word. Agree a family code word, keep it secret, and make sure every family member who might receive a call knows it. Practise asking for it once, so it feels normal.
  2. Call-back rule. No money, gift cards, or personal details are sent on the basis of a single call or message. Hang up, call back on a number already saved, and confirm through a second channel.
  3. No secrecy rule. No family member will ever be asked to keep a money request secret from the rest of the family. A request that demands secrecy is treated as unverified until checked.
  4. Trusted numbers list. Keep an up-to-date list of the phone numbers and contact details you trust for each family member, in every phone, and on a card by the home phone for older relatives.
  5. Payment pause. Any urgent payment request waits at least an hour, and is checked with a second family member, before it is acted on. Urgency does not override the pause.
  6. Privacy check. Review social media privacy settings together, once, and agree what is shared publicly. Keep voicemail greetings short, and avoid posting high-resolution portraits and long voice recordings publicly where possible.
  7. Work rule. At work, no transfer, change of bank details, or password reset is authorised on a single call or message. Every urgent request needs a second confirmation through a known channel.
  8. Reporting plan. Agree in advance who to call first if a scam is suspected — the bank, the platform, and the fraud-reporting authority — and keep those numbers with the plan.

The plan is deliberately short. A long, complicated plan will not be remembered in a stressful moment, and a plan that lives only in one person's head will not help the person who receives the call. Keep it to one page, keep the language plain, and talk through it together, including with children and older relatives. The Bottom Line

Deepfake scams succeed because they borrow trust: a familiar voice, a familiar face, an urgent story, and pressure to act before you check. The defence is not learning to spot every fake, because no visual check and no detector is 100% reliable, and a good fake can pass them all. The defence is a habit: pause, ask for the family code word, ask an unscripted question, hang up and call back on a number you already trust, and never send money, gift cards, or personal details on the basis of a single call or message. Agree the code word today, agree the call-back rule today, and talk it through with the children and older relatives in your family, so that the check is ready before the call comes. A two-minute verification will never harm a genuine emergency. It will, very often, stop a scam. And if a call ever feels wrong — too urgent, too secret, too insistent on a payment method that cannot be reversed — trust that feeling, hang up, and check. The real person will understand. The scammer is counting on you not to.
