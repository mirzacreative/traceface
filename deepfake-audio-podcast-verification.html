Deepfake Audio in Podcasts: Check Before You Trust It

November 26, 2026 · Muhammad Atif Iqbal

A short clip circulates: a well-known voice, in a familiar podcast style, apparently saying something startling — an admission, an endorsement, a claim about another person, a prediction about money or politics. The clip is thirty seconds long. It has captions. It has music underneath. It has been reposted by accounts you have never heard of, each adding a stronger caption than the last. The question it raises is not whether the voice sounds convincing. A convincing voice is the starting point of the problem, not evidence that the clip is genuine. The question is whether the clip can be traced to a real episode, heard in full, and checked against what the speaker actually said, in context, on the speaker's own channels. This guide explains that check, step by step, for viral podcast and interview clips. It is about published recordings — podcasts, interviews, and talk-show segments that are reposted as short clips — not about private voice messages or phone calls. Our guide to spotting an AI-cloned voice covers the voice itself, our guide on deepfake scam protection covers a different situation, in which a voice is used to ask for money or information, and our guide on how to report a deepfake covers what to do when a harmful recording needs to be removed. This guide starts where those guides leave off: a clip is circulating, it claims to be from a podcast or an interview, and it needs to be checked before it is trusted or shared. No method in this guide is presented as 100% reliable, and no detection check is presented as a verdict. The source check — the original episode, in full — is the strongest evidence available, and the guide is honest about what even that check cannot prove. It is general information only, not legal advice.

1. Why Podcast and Interview Clips Are Easy to Fake — and Easy to Clip

Podcasts and interviews are unusually vulnerable to manipulation, for reasons that have little to do with technology and much to do with how the format works. First, there is a large supply of training material. A public figure who has recorded hours of podcasts, interviews, and speeches has, in effect, published a large library of their own voice, in many moods, on many topics, which can be used to build a convincing imitation, and which also means that almost any sentence, cut from its context, can be made to sound like part of a conversation. Second, the format is conversational and unscripted, so listeners expect hesitation, interruption, laughter, and half-finished sentences, and a manipulated clip that contains those features does not sound wrong to a listener who is used to them. Third, the clip economy rewards brevity. A podcast episode is an hour long, but a clip is thirty seconds, and the thirty seconds that circulate are the thirty seconds that were most surprising, most quotable, or most easily misunderstood, chosen by someone whose interest is in the reaction, not in the accuracy of the excerpt.

That third reason is the one most often missed, because it does not require any fake audio at all. A genuine recording, cut in the right places, can mislead as effectively as a synthesised one. A question can be removed, so that an answer sounds like a statement made unprompted. A qualifier — "if", "suppose", "some people say" — can be cut, so that a hypothetical sounds like a claim. An answer to one question can be joined to a different question, so that the speaker appears to be answering a question they were never asked. A pause can be shortened, so that a considered answer sounds like a snap judgement. None of those edits needs a voice clone. Each needs only a pair of cuts, and each produces a clip that sounds, to a listener who has not heard the original, exactly like a real moment from a real conversation, because, in a narrow sense, it is: the words were spoken, by that voice, at some point, in some order. The check in this guide therefore has two jobs, not one. It must ask whether the audio is genuine, and it must ask whether the genuine audio has been cut so that it means something different from what the speaker meant. A clip that passes the first question can still fail the second, and a clip that fails the second should not be trusted or shared, even if every word in it was genuinely spoken.

2. Start With the Clip Itself: What It Shows, and What It Hides

Before searching for the original, take a minute to describe the clip as it stands, because the description will guide the search and will become the record if the clip needs to be reported later. Note the link to the post, the account that posted it, the date and time, and the caption, exactly as written, including any claim the caption makes about the clip. Note the length of the clip, and whether it is presented as a complete answer, a complete exchange, or an excerpt. Note whether the clip names the podcast, the episode, the interviewer, or the date of the recording, and whether it links to the original. Note whether the audio has music, captions, or effects added over it, and whether the video, where there is one, matches the audio: lip movement that does not match the words, a cut in the picture at a point where the audio changes, or a still image used as a background for a long passage are all reasons to check further, though none of them, on its own, proves the audio is fake. A clip that names its source and links to it is easier to check than a clip that names no source, and a clip that names no source should be treated with more caution, not less, because the absence of a source is itself information.

Listen to the clip, once, in full, with headphones if you can, and note, in your own words, what the speaker appears to be saying, and what the caption claims the speaker is saying. The two are often different. A caption may claim that a speaker admitted something, endorsed something, or predicted something, while the audio, listened to carefully, contains a question, a quotation, a joke, or a hypothetical, and the gap between the caption and the audio is the first sign that the clip needs checking. Note any point where the audio changes suddenly: a change in room sound, a change in loudness, a word that sounds cut off, a breath that disappears, or a background noise that starts or stops without reason. Those are edit points, and edit points are where meaning is most often changed, whether by cutting or by synthesis. Do not treat an edit point as proof of fakery, because genuine clips are edited too, for length, for clarity, and for the clip format itself. Treat an edit point as a place to listen again, in the original, in full context, and to ask what was removed, and why. Keep the note short and factual. It is the starting point for the search, not a conclusion.

3. Find the Original Episode

The strongest check available is also the simplest to describe: find the original podcast episode or interview, on the show's own channel or on the publisher's own platform, and listen to the passage in full. Start with the name of the show, the name of the speaker, and the distinctive words of the clip, searched together, in a search engine and in the podcast platform's own search. Search for the distinctive phrase, in quotation marks, because a distinctive phrase is more likely to appear in a transcript, a description, or a report of the episode than a common phrase is. Search for the episode by date, if the clip names a date, and by guest, if the clip names a guest. Check the show's own channel first — its website, its podcast feed, its video channel — before trusting a re-upload, because a re-upload can be edited, retitled, or misdescribed, while the show's own channel is the source the re-upload claims to copy. If the show publishes transcripts, search the transcript for the distinctive phrase. If it publishes chapter markers or timestamps, use them to find the passage quickly, and then listen beyond the passage, before and after it, so that the context is heard, not only the passage itself.

If the original cannot be found, that fact matters, and it should change how the clip is treated. A genuine clip from a real, published podcast should usually be findable, by show, by speaker, and by phrase, on the show's own channel, and a clip that cannot be found anywhere except as a short re-upload, with no episode named, no date, and no link, has not passed the source check. That does not prove the clip is fake: the episode may be behind a paywall, may have been removed, may be described in different words, or may be hard to find because the search terms are wrong. It does mean the clip should not be trusted or shared on the strength of the clip alone, because the evidence that would support it — the original episode — has not been found. Try the search again with different words, try the show's archive by date, and try the speaker's own channels, as the next section explains, before concluding. If the original still cannot be found, say so, in words, if the clip is discussed at all: "This clip claims to be from this show, but the original episode could not be found," is an honest description, and it is more useful than a share with a caption that treats the claim as fact. A clip that cannot be traced to a source is a claim, not a recording, and it should be treated as one.

When the original is found, compare the clip with the original, passage by passage. Does the clip's audio appear in the episode, in the same words, in the same order? Does the question that precedes the answer in the episode match the question the clip implies? Does the speaker's next sentence, in the episode, qualify, correct, or contradict the sentence the clip ends on? Does the clip join two passages that are minutes apart in the episode, presenting them as one continuous answer? Does the clip remove a laugh, a pause, or a correction that changes the tone? Listen to at least a minute before the passage and a minute after it, and read the episode description, because the description often states the topic, the date, and the context in which the passage was recorded. If the clip matches the original, in words, in order, and in context, the clip has passed the strongest check available, and it can be described as a genuine excerpt, with a link to the original. If it does not match, note exactly how it differs — the words cut, the order changed, the question removed, the passage joined — because the difference is the evidence, and the evidence should be described precisely, not summarised as "fake" when the problem is editing, or as "edited" when the problem is synthesis.

4. Full Context vs Clipped Audio: Listening for What Was Removed

The full-context check is the heart of the method, because it is the check that catches the most common form of misleading podcast audio: the genuine clip, cut to change its meaning. Listen for the question. In an interview, the meaning of an answer depends on the question, and a clip that removes the question removes the meaning. A speaker who says "Yes, I would" in answer to "Would you consider it if the conditions changed?" is saying something different from a speaker who says "Yes, I would" as an unprompted statement, and a clip that presents the answer without the question is misleading, even if the answer is genuine. Listen for the qualifier. A speaker who says "Some people argue that..." is reporting an argument, not making it, and a clip that cuts the opening words presents a report as a claim. Listen for the tense and the subject. A speaker who says "He said that he would..." is quoting someone else, and a clip that cuts the attribution presents a quotation as the speaker's own view. Listen for the joke. A speaker who laughs, whose interviewer laughs, or who says "I am joking" before or after a remark is not making a serious claim, and a clip that cuts the laugh and the disclaimer presents a joke as a statement.

Listen, too, for the passage that follows. A speaker who corrects themselves, who adds a condition, or who returns to a point later in the episode to clarify it, is giving the listener the full meaning, and a clip that ends before the correction is incomplete. A speaker who is interrupted, who is asked to clarify, or who is challenged by the interviewer is in a conversation, and a clip that presents one side of the conversation as a monologue is incomplete. The test is simple: would a listener who heard only the clip understand the passage in the same way as a listener who heard the full exchange? If the answer is no, the clip is misleadingly edited, whatever the intention behind the edit, and it should not be shared as if it were the full exchange. If the clip is shared at all, it should be shared with the link to the original, with the context stated, and with the edit described, so that the next listener can hear the full passage. A clip shared with its context is a quotation. A clip shared without its context is a claim, and the person sharing it has taken responsibility for the claim, whether they intended to or not.

5. Cross-Check With the Speaker's Own Channels

A second, independent check is to look at the speaker's own channels: the speaker's own social media accounts, website, or newsletter, the show's own accounts, and the interviewer's accounts, to see whether the speaker or the show has confirmed, denied, or commented on the clip. A speaker who is quoted as saying something startling will often, though not always, have said something about it, or will have posted the episode itself, with a description that states the topic. A show that published the episode will usually have posted it, with a title and a description, and the title and description are evidence of what the episode was about, as the show itself understood it. Search the speaker's name, with the distinctive phrase from the clip, on the speaker's own accounts, and search the show's accounts for the episode, by date and by guest. If the speaker has denied the clip, or has posted the full episode with a description that contradicts the clip's caption, that is strong evidence against the clip, and it should be noted, with the link. If the speaker has confirmed the clip, or has reposted it, that is evidence in its favour, though it does not remove the need to hear the full context, because a speaker may confirm the words while the clip still cuts the context.

Be careful, in this check, to use the speaker's genuine channels, not an impersonating account. An account that uses the speaker's name and photograph, that posts the clip with a caption claiming to be the speaker, or that claims the clip is genuine, is not the speaker's own channel, and it should not be treated as one. Check for the account the speaker links to from their own website, for the account that is verified where verification is offered, and for the account that has a history of posts consistent with the speaker's known work. Our guide to reporting a deepfake explains the impersonation problem in more detail, and the same caution applies here: a fake account confirming a fake clip proves nothing. And be careful with silence. A speaker who has said nothing about a clip has not confirmed it, and has not denied it. Silence may mean the speaker has not seen the clip, has chosen not to engage with it, or is taking advice. Silence is not evidence, in either direction, and it should not be described as confirmation or as denial. The cross-check is useful when it produces a statement, a post, or a link. When it produces nothing, the source check — the original episode — remains the check that matters.

Cross-check, too, with independent reporting, where it exists. A startling claim in a podcast, if genuine, will often be reported by news outlets, by other podcasts, or by commentators who heard the episode, with a link to the episode and a quotation in context. Search for the claim, in the words of the clip, in news and in the show's coverage, and note whether the reporting quotes the passage in full, whether it links to the episode, and whether it describes the context. Independent reporting that quotes the passage in full, with a link, supports the clip. Independent reporting that describes the clip as edited, or that quotes a denial, counts against it. Reporting that merely repeats the clip, without a link to the original, without a quotation in full, and without a check of its own, is not independent evidence. It is the same clip, repeated, and it should be counted as one source, not as several. A claim that has been repeated by many accounts, each citing the previous account, is a chain of repetition, not a chain of evidence, and the chain should be followed back to the first link that can be checked: the original episode.

6. Audio-Consistency Tells: Useful Signals, Never Proof

Listening for audio-consistency tells — the small, technical signs that a recording has been edited or synthesised — can raise suspicion and can guide the source check, but no tell, on its own, proves a clip is real or fake. The tells below are therefore presented as reasons to check further, not as verdicts, and they should always be combined with the source check. A clip that shows several tells, and that cannot be found in the original episode, is a clip to treat with serious caution. A clip that shows no tells, but that cannot be found in the original episode, is still a clip that has not passed the source check, and a clip that shows a tell, but that matches the original episode in full context, is a clip whose tell has an innocent explanation, such as ordinary editing for length.

Room sound and background noise. A genuine recording made in one place, in one session, usually has a consistent room sound: the same faint hum, the same echo, the same background noise, throughout. A clip in which the room sound changes suddenly, in which background noise starts or stops at a cut, or in which one sentence sounds as if it were recorded in a different room from the next, may have been assembled from different recordings, or may have been edited at that point. Ordinary editing can also change room sound, where a passage has been cut, so a change is a reason to listen to the original, not a conclusion. Pacing, breath, and natural pauses. Natural speech has breaths, pauses, hesitations, and changes of pace, and a speaker's pace usually varies with the thought being expressed. A passage in which the pace is unnaturally even, in which breaths are absent where they would be expected, or in which a speaker moves from one complex sentence to the next without a pause, may have been assembled, or may have been generated. Again, editing for length can remove pauses, so the tell is a prompt to check, not a proof. Pronunciation and emphasis. A speaker usually pronounces names, places, and technical terms consistently, and usually stresses the words that carry the meaning. A passage in which a familiar name is pronounced differently from the speaker's usual pronunciation, in which the stress falls on the wrong word, or in which a word is pronounced with an accent the speaker does not usually use, is worth checking against the original. A single mispronunciation, on its own, proves nothing: speakers mispronounce words, especially when tired or speaking quickly.

Interview dynamics. In a genuine interview, the interviewer and the guest respond to each other: the interviewer asks, the guest answers, the interviewer follows up, the guest laughs, interrupts, or pauses. A clip in which the guest's answer does not fit the interviewer's question, in which the interviewer does not react to a startling claim, or in which the conversation jumps from one topic to another without a bridge, may have been assembled from different parts of the episode, or from different episodes. Listen for the interviewer's side of the exchange, in the original, because the interviewer's reaction is often the clearest evidence of what was actually said, and when. Music, captions, and added effects. A clip with music, sound effects, or captions added over the audio is harder to judge by ear, because the additions mask the room sound, the breaths, and the edit points. A clip that is presented with dramatic music, with captions that state a claim the audio does not, or with effects that emphasise a single word, should be checked in the original, without the additions, before it is trusted. The additions are not evidence of fakery, because genuine clips are often presented with music and captions for the clip format, but they are a reason to insist on hearing the unadorned original. Our guide to spotting an AI-cloned voice covers the voice tells in more detail, and the same caution applies there: a tell is a reason to check, not a verdict.

7. Transcripts, Quotes, and the Written Record

Where a transcript exists, it is a useful second record, but it should be treated with the same caution as the audio. A transcript published by the show, or by a reputable outlet that heard the episode, can be searched for the distinctive phrase, and it can show the question, the answer, and the passage that follows, in writing, which is easier to compare than audio alone. A transcript generated automatically, or published by an account that reposted the clip, may contain errors, may be based on the clip rather than on the episode, and may not be a reliable record of the original. Check who published the transcript, whether it covers the whole episode or only the clipped passage, and whether it matches the audio you hear in the original. A quotation in a news report, in a book, or in the speaker's own writing, that matches the clip, supports it. A quotation that differs from the clip, in wording or in context, counts against the clip's caption, even if the words in the clip are genuine, because the difference shows that the clip's presentation, not only its audio, needs checking.

Be careful, too, with quotations that are presented as text over a clip, or as a caption that paraphrases the audio. A text quotation may be accurate, may be paraphrased, or may be invented, and the audio should be checked against it, word by word, where the claim matters. If the text quotation contains a sentence that does not appear in the audio, or if the audio contains a qualifier that the text quotation omits, the text is not a reliable record of the audio, and the audio, in the original, is the record that matters. Keep a note of the exact words, in the original, with a timestamp, where the claim is important, because a precise quotation, with a link and a timestamp, is the evidence that allows another person to check the claim for themselves, and it is the evidence that should be given if the clip is discussed, reported, or corrected. A paraphrase, however vivid, is not a quotation, and it should not be presented as one.

8. Technical Checks: Provenance, Metadata, and Detection Tools

Technical checks can add evidence, but they cannot replace the source check, and their limits should be stated plainly. Provenance information, where it exists, can show the origin of a file: the C2PA Content Credentials system, for example, attaches signed information about a file's origin and its edit history, where the camera, tool, or platform supports it, and a file that carries valid credentials can be traced to its source more reliably than a file that carries none. The absence of credentials, however, proves nothing: most audio and video published today carries no C2PA information, and a genuine clip without credentials is still genuine. Metadata — the information embedded in a file about when it was created, with what device or software — can be useful where the original file is available, but metadata is easily removed, easily changed, and usually lost when a file is re-uploaded to a social platform, so a reposted clip will often carry no useful metadata at all. Do not upload a private or sensitive audio file to a search engine or a detection tool without considering the privacy implications, as our guide to verifying a photo's origin with reverse image search explains for images, and the same caution applies to audio.

Detection tools — software that analyses audio for signs of synthesis or manipulation — can raise or lower suspicion, and they are most useful as one input among several. A tool that flags a clip should prompt a source check, not a conclusion, and a tool that clears a clip should not remove the need for a source check, because a misleadingly edited genuine clip will often clear a synthesis detector, and a genuine clip that has been compressed, re-uploaded, or edited for length may be flagged for reasons that have nothing to do with fakery. Where a tool is used, note the tool, the result, and the limits of the tool, and do not present the result as a percentage of certainty, or as proof, unless the tool itself states its limits in those terms and the statement is quoted accurately. No detection method is 100% reliable, and no tool result, on its own, proves a clip is real or fake. The combination — source check, full-context check, cross-check with the speaker's channels, and, where available, a technical check — is stronger than any single check, and the combination should be described honestly, with each piece of evidence stated, and with the gaps stated too. A check that says "the original could not be found, the speaker has not commented, and a tool flagged the clip" is an honest check, and it is more useful than a check that says "the clip is fake" on the strength of one tool alone.

9. What to Do Before Sharing: A Short Routine

Before sharing a viral podcast or interview clip, run a short routine, in order, and share only if the routine passes. 1. Note the facts. The link, the account, the date, the caption, and what the clip appears to claim, in your own words. 2. Find the original. Search by show, speaker, and distinctive phrase, on the show's own channel, and listen to the passage in full, with a minute before and a minute after. 3. Check the context. Ask whether a listener who heard only the clip would understand the passage as a listener who heard the full exchange would. If not, the clip is misleadingly edited, and it should not be shared as the full exchange. 4. Cross-check the speaker's channels. Look for a confirmation, a denial, or a post of the episode itself, from the speaker's genuine channels, and for independent reporting that quotes the passage in full, with a link. 5. Listen for tells, and use technical checks as prompts, not verdicts. Note any edit points, changes in room sound, or mismatches with the interview dynamic, and treat them as reasons to check further. 6. Decide. Share only if the original supports the clip, in words and in context, and share it with the link to the original, so that the next listener can check it too. If the original cannot be found, or if the context contradicts the caption, do not share the clip, and do not repeat its claim as fact.

If the clip is harmful — if it defames a person, if it is being used to mislead voters, customers, or investors, if it places a person in a false and damaging light, or if it is part of a pattern of harassment — the response is not only to refrain from sharing, but to consider reporting it, following the steps in our guide on how to report a deepfake: preserve the evidence, report the content where it appears, tell the person affected through a trusted channel, and escalate if removal stalls. Do not repost the clip to warn about it, because reposting spreads the audio to people who would not otherwise have heard it, and creates new copies that will themselves need to be reported. Warn in words, privately, without the file: "A clip claiming to be from this show is circulating on this platform, posted by this account, on this date. I checked for the original episode and could not find it" — or, where the original was found, "I found the original, and the clip cuts the question and the correction that follow." That message warns the person, preserves the evidence, and does not spread the content. If the clip is being used to ask for money, for login details, or for personal information, the response is the different routine in our scam protection guide, which is not repeated here, and the clip should not be acted on until it has been checked through the source routine above.

10. If You Have Already Shared a Clip That Turns Out to Be Misleading

Sharing a misleading clip, in good faith, is a common mistake, and the remedy is straightforward: correct it, promptly, in the same place, with the same prominence as the original share, and with the evidence. Delete or edit the original post where the platform allows it, and post a correction that states what was shared, what the check found, and where the original episode can be heard, with a link. Do not delete the correction, do not quietly remove the post without explanation where people have already seen and repeated it, and do not defend the share on the grounds that the clip "sounded real" or that "many people shared it". The correction should be factual and brief: "I shared a clip claiming X. I checked the original episode, and the clip cuts the question and the correction that follow. The full passage is here, with a link." A correction of that kind restores trust, and it gives the people who saw the original share a chance to correct their own understanding. A share that is left uncorrected, after the check has shown it to be misleading, becomes a knowing share, and it does more harm than the original mistake.

Correcting a share also means telling the people who were told directly. If the clip was sent to a group, a chat, or a colleague, with a claim attached, send the correction to the same group, chat, or colleague, with the same directness, and with the link to the original. If the clip was used as the basis for a decision — a purchase, a vote, a hiring decision, a public statement — revisit the decision, with the full context, and say so, where the decision affects others. The habit to build is not never to make a mistake. It is to check before sharing, to correct promptly when a check shows a mistake, and to keep the record — the link, the date, the check, and the correction — so that the next clip is checked more quickly, and shared more carefully. A person who corrects a share promptly, with evidence, will be trusted with the next claim. A person who defends a share after it has been shown to be misleading will not, and the next claim, however well checked, will be received with doubt.

The Bottom Line

To check a viral podcast or interview clip before you trust it, find the original episode on the show's own channel, listen to the passage in full context, with the question, the qualifier, and the passage that follows, and cross-check the claim with the speaker's own channels and with independent reporting that quotes the passage in full. Listen for audio-consistency tells — changes in room sound, pacing, breath, and interview dynamics — as reasons to check further, never as proof, and use technical checks, including provenance and detection tools, as one input among several, with their limits stated. Share only if the original supports the clip, in words and in context, and share it with the link to the original, so that the next listener can check it too. If the original cannot be found, or if the context contradicts the caption, do not share the clip, and do not repeat its claim as fact. If the clip is harmful, preserve the evidence and follow our guide on how to report a deepfake. For the voice itself, read our guide to spotting an AI-cloned voice, for provenance, read our guide to C2PA Content Credentials, and for origin checks on images that accompany a clip, read our guide to reverse image search. No detection method is 100% reliable, and no single check proves a clip is real or fake. The source check, the full-context check, and the cross-check, together, are the strongest evidence available, and they are the checks to run before a clip is trusted, shared, or acted on. Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a podcast clip is deepfake audio?

No single check can prove a clip is real or fake. Start with the source: find the original podcast episode on the show's own channel, check whether the clip appears there, and listen to the passage in full context. Cross-check the claim with the speaker's own channels and with independent reporting of the same interview. Listen for audio-consistency tells — abrupt changes in room sound, pacing, breath, or background noise at edit points — but treat them as reasons to check further, not as proof. A clip that cannot be found in the original episode, or that only exists as a short re-upload, should not be trusted or shared on the strength of the clip alone.

What is the difference between a clipped real recording and a fake recording?

A clipped real recording uses genuine audio, cut so that it changes the meaning: a sentence removed from its question, an answer joined to a different question, or a pause or qualifier cut out. A fake recording uses synthesised or manipulated audio to put words in a speaker's mouth that the speaker never said. Both can mislead in the same way, and both are checked the same way: by finding the full original and listening to the passage in context. The distinction matters for what you say about the clip afterwards: describe a clipped recording as misleadingly edited, and a synthesised recording as fake, only when the evidence supports that description, and say what you do not know when it does not.

What should I do before sharing a viral podcast or interview clip?

Before sharing, find the original episode, check the full context of the passage, and cross-check the claim with the speaker's own channels. If you cannot find the original, or if the clip's meaning depends on a passage you cannot hear in full, do not share it, and do not repeat its claim as fact. If you need to warn someone, describe the clip in words, without reposting the audio, and say what you checked and what remains unverified. Keep a note of the link, the account that posted the clip, and the date, so that the clip can be reported if it is harmful, following the steps in the reporting guide linked in this post.

Can an audio detection tool prove a podcast clip is a deepfake?

No detection tool can prove, on its own, that a clip is real or fake. Detection tools and technical checks can raise or lower suspicion, and they are most useful when combined with source checks: the original episode, the speaker's own channels, and independent reporting. A tool result should be treated as one piece of evidence, with its limits stated, not as a verdict. If a tool flags a clip, check the source before concluding the clip is fake, and if a tool clears a clip, still check the source before concluding it is genuine, because a genuine-sounding clip can still be misleadingly edited.
